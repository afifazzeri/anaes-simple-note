1) HYPERTENSION CRISIS (URGENCY EMERGENCY) (CPG 2018)

==========================================
HYPERTENSIVE URGENCY – QUICK REFERENCE SUMMARY
==========================================

-------------------------------------------------
DEFINITION
-------------------------------------------------
• Severe BP elevation (≥180/110 mmHg)  
• **No acute end-organ damage**  
• May include grade III–IV retinal changes (accelerated/malignant hypertension)  
• No symptoms/signs of acute target organ injury  

-------------------------------------------------
AIM OF MANAGEMENT
-------------------------------------------------
• Controlled, predictable, and safe BP reduction  
• Avoid rapid fall → prevent coronary, cerebral, or renal ischemia  
• Target: **≈25% reduction in BP over 24 hours**  
• Do not reduce below **160/100 mmHg** initially  
• **IV antihypertensives NOT indicated**

-------------------------------------------------
POSSIBLE PRECIPITATING FACTORS
-------------------------------------------------
• Non-adherence to medications  
• Poor outpatient BP control  
• Acute pain  
• Herbal supplements  
• Emotional stress  

-------------------------------------------------
MANAGEMENT STEPS (Figure 6-A)
-------------------------------------------------
1. Confirm **BP ≥180/110 mmHg**  
2. **Rest in quiet room** (no talking or active listening)  
   - Check BP every 30 minutes for up to 2 hours  
3. **If BP reduces by 10–20 mmHg systolic → Responder**
   - Discharge with hypertensive urgency discharge plan  
4. **If no BP reduction → Non-responder**
   - Start oral antihypertensive  
   - Recheck BP every 30 minutes for 1 hour  
   - If BP reduces → Discharge  
   - If still no reduction → Admit or refer for further care  

-------------------------------------------------
ORAL DRUG OPTIONS (Table 6-B)
-------------------------------------------------
**Captopril**
- Dose: 12.5 mg  
- Onset: 0.5 hr  
- Duration: 6 hr  
- Repeat: every 1–2 hr (PRN)

**Nifedipine**
- Dose: 10 mg  
- Onset: 0.5 hr  
- Duration: 3–5 hr  
- Repeat: every 1–2 hr (PRN)

**Labetalol**
- Dose: 200 mg  
- Onset: 2 hr  
- Duration: 6 hr  
- Repeat: every 4 hr (PRN)

-------------------------------------------------
HYPERTENSIVE URGENCY DISCHARGE PLAN (Figure 6-B)
-------------------------------------------------
**Blood Pressure Monitoring**
- Home BP monitoring or clinic check ≥3 times/week  
- If BP >180/110 mmHg:
  - Repeat after 5 min  
  - If still high or symptomatic → Seek medical help  

**Medication**
- Continue antihypertensives as prescribed  

**Follow-up**
- Attend scheduled clinic review  

**When to Call Emergency (999)**
- Chest pain  
- Difficulty breathing  
- Altered mental status  

-------------------------------------------------
KEY NOTES
-------------------------------------------------
• Combination oral therapy may be required.  
• No role for IV medications.  
• Many cases related to medication withdrawal or poor adherence.  
• Continuous monitoring during observation period is essential.


HYPERTENSIVE EMERGENCY (CPG 2018)
-------------------------------------------------
DEFINITION
-------------------------------------------------
• Severe elevation of BP associated with **acute or progressive end-organ damage**.  
• Common complications:
  - Acute heart failure  
  - Aortic dissection  
  - Acute coronary syndromes  
  - Hypertensive encephalopathy  
  - Acute renal failure  
  - Subarachnoid haemorrhage  
  - Intracranial haemorrhage  
• May occur even with BP <180/110 mmHg if BP rises rapidly.

-------------------------------------------------
GENERAL MANAGEMENT PRINCIPLES
-------------------------------------------------
• **Immediate hospital admission** for monitoring and intervention.  
• **Rapid BP reduction** guided by clinical context:
  - Target reduction: **10–25% within minutes to hours**.  
  - Do **not reduce below 160/90 mmHg** initially.  
• **Parenteral antihypertensive agents** are preferred (see Table 6-D).  
• Select drug based on:
  - Pharmacologic properties  
  - Underlying pathophysiology  
  - Degree of organ damage  
  - Desired rate of BP reduction  
  - Patient comorbidities  

-------------------------------------------------
EVIDENCE & DRUG CHOICE
-------------------------------------------------
• Few direct comparative trials available.  
• Meta-analysis: **IV labetalol** and **IV nicardipine** show similar efficacy and safety.  
• **Nicardipine** provides more predictable and consistent BP control.  

-------------------------------------------------
SPECIFIC CLINICAL SCENARIOS
-------------------------------------------------
• **Aortic dissection**
  - Reduce SBP to **<140 mmHg within the first hour**,  
  - Preferably to **<120 mmHg** if tolerated.  

• **Severe preeclampsia/eclampsia**
  - Rapidly reduce BP to **<140 mmHg** during first hour.  

• **Pheochromocytoma crisis**
  - Same goal: **SBP <140 mmHg** within the first hour.  

-------------------------------------------------
THERAPEUTIC GOALS
-------------------------------------------------
• Achieve controlled, safe, and rapid BP reduction.  
• Prevent further **target organ damage**.  
• Maintain adequate organ perfusion.  
• Prioritize **early recognition** and **prompt initiation** of appropriate therapy.  

-------------------------------------------------
FLOW (Figure 6-C)
-------------------------------------------------
1. Confirm **SBP ≥180 mmHg and/or DBP ≥110 mmHg**.  
2. Assess for **acute target organ damage/complication**:
   - If **Yes → Hypertensive Emergency**
   - If **No → Hypertensive Urgency**
3. For emergency:
   - If **aortic dissection, eclampsia/preeclampsia, pheochromocytoma crisis →**
     - Reduce SBP **<140 mmHg within 1 hour** (or <120 mmHg for dissection)
   - If **other conditions →**
     - Reduce BP by **10–25% in first hour**
     - Then target ≤160/100 mmHg within 6 hours
     - Gradual normalization over 24–48 hours

-------------------------------------------------
KEY TAKEAWAYS
-------------------------------------------------
• **Hypertensive emergency = BP + end-organ damage.**  
• **IV therapy required.**  
• Avoid excessive rapid reduction to prevent ischemia.  
• Tailor treatment to the **clinical scenario** and **organ involvement.**

TREATMENT OPTIONS FOR HYPERTENSIVE EMERGENCIES – QUICK REFERENCE
==================================================================

-------------------------------------------------
GENERAL PRINCIPLES
-------------------------------------------------
• **Avoid rapid BP reduction** — may cause ischemic events (e.g., cerebral, coronary, renal).  
• **Do not use** sublingual fast-acting nifedipine — linked to severe adverse events.  
• **Preferred** oral nifedipine retard for hypertensive urgency (slow and controlled effect).  
• **Goal:** Controlled BP reduction tailored to clinical condition.  

-------------------------------------------------
HYPERTENSIVE URGENCY
-------------------------------------------------
• Rest for 2 hours; aim **SBP ↓ by 10–20 mmHg**.  
• If not achieved → initiate oral therapy.  
• Combination oral therapy: **BP ↓ ~25% within 24 hrs.**

-------------------------------------------------
HYPERTENSIVE EMERGENCY
-------------------------------------------------
• Use **IV antihypertensives**.  
• **BP reduction targets:**
  - ↓ by **≤25% in first hour**.  
  - If stable → **160/100 mmHg within 2–6 hours**.  
  - Gradual normalization over 24–48 hrs.  
• **Specific targets:**
  - Severe preeclampsia/eclampsia → SBP <140 mmHg (1 hr)  
  - Pheochromocytoma crisis → SBP <140 mmHg (1 hr)  
  - Aortic dissection → SBP <120 mmHg (1 hr)

-------------------------------------------------
INTRAVENOUS DRUG OPTIONS (TABLE 6-D)
-------------------------------------------------

**1. Labetalol**
- **Adult:** 20 mg IV slowly (≥2 min), repeat 40–80 mg every 10 min (max 200 mg).  
- **Child:** 0.25–0.5 mg/kg IV (max 20 mg); IVI 0.5–1 mg/kg/hr (maintain 0.25–3 mg/kg/hr).  
- **Onset:** ≤5 min.  
- **Duration:** 3–6 hrs.  
- **Remarks:** Supine during and 3 hrs post-procedure; caution in heart failure.  

**2. Nitroglycerine**
- **Adult:** Start 5–25 mcg/min; usual 10–200 mcg/min; up to 400 mcg/min.  
- **Onset:** 2–5 min.  
- **Duration:** 3–5 hrs.  
- **Remarks:** Preferred for ACS and acute pulmonary oedema.  

**3. Isosorbide Dinitrate**
- **Adult:** IV 2–20 mg/hr; titrate to target BP.  
- **Onset:** 3–15 min.  
- **Duration:** ~1 hr.  
- **Remarks:** Preferred in coronary syndrome.  

**4. Hydralazine**
- **Adult:** 5–10 mg slow IV; repeat after 20–30 min or continuous infusion (0.2–0.3 mg/min; maintain 0.05–0.15 mg/min).  
- **Child:** 0.1–0.5 mg/kg IV (max 10 mg), repeat q4–6h; IVI 12.5–50 mcg/kg/hr (max 3 mg/kg/day).  
- **Onset:** 10–30 min.  
- **Duration:** 3–8 hrs.  
- **Remarks:** Caution in ACS, CVA, or aortic dissection; unpredictable BP effects.  

**5. Nicardipine**
- **Adult:** IV 5 mg/hr; titrate up to 15 mg/hr, then reduce to 3 mg/hr after response.  
- **Child:** Bolus 0.5–5.5 mcg/kg over 1 min; IVI 1–4 mcg/kg/min.  
- **Onset:** 5–10 min.  
- **Duration:** 1–4 hrs.  
- **Remarks:** Caution in heart failure and coronary ischemia.  

**6. Esmolol**
- **Adult:** 80 mg loading over 15–30 sec; infusion 150 mcg/kg/min (up to 300 mcg/kg/min).  
- **Child:** 250–500 mcg/kg bolus; IVI 50–200 mcg/kg/min for 4 min; may repeat.  
- **Onset:** 1 min.  
- **Duration:** 10–20 min.  
- **Remarks:** For perioperative use and tachyarrhythmias.  

**7. Sodium Nitroprusside**
- **Adult:** 0.3–1.5 mcg/kg/min; usual 0.5–6 mcg/kg/min; max 8 mcg/kg/min; discontinue if no response after 10 min.  
- **Child:** 0.25–0.5 mcg/kg/min; may double every 15–20 min (max 6 mcg/kg/min).  
- **Onset:** Seconds.  
- **Duration:** 1–5 min.  
- **Remarks:**  
  - Requires intra-arterial BP monitoring.  
  - Caution in heart failure, elderly, and those on antihypertensives.  

-------------------------------------------------
KEY TAKEAWAYS
-------------------------------------------------
• Avoid rapid or excessive BP lowering to prevent ischemia.  
• Use **parenteral therapy** only for hypertensive emergencies.  
• **Monitor continuously** – especially if using nitroprusside or beta-blockers.  
• **Tailor therapy** to underlying pathology (e.g., ACS, stroke, dissection).  
• **Transition to oral agents** after stabilization for long-term BP control.





2) POST CARDIAC ARREST ASSESSMENT 
===========================================
ADULT POST–CARDIAC ARREST CARE (AHA 2020) – QUICK REFERENCE
===========================================

-------------------------------------------------
INITIAL STABILIZATION PHASE
-------------------------------------------------
• **Airway management**
  - Confirm endotracheal tube placement (capnography/capnometry).
  - Consider early placement of ETT.

• **Respiratory management**
  - Start at 10 breaths/min.
  - Target SpO₂ 92–98%.
  - Maintain PaCO₂ 35–45 mmHg.

• **Hemodynamic management**
  - Maintain systolic BP >90 mmHg.
  - Maintain MAP >65 mmHg.
  - Use crystalloids and/or vasopressors/inotropes as needed.

• **12-lead ECG**
  - Obtain immediately after ROSC.

-------------------------------------------------
CARDIAC INTERVENTION CONSIDERATION
-------------------------------------------------
• Emergent intervention if:
  - STEMI present.
  - Unstable cardiogenic shock.
  - Mechanical circulatory support required.

-------------------------------------------------
NEUROLOGICAL ASSESSMENT
-------------------------------------------------
• Assess for command following.

→ **If following commands:**  
  - Manage as *Awake – Other critical care management.*

→ **If not following commands (Comatose):**  
  - Initiate Targeted Temperature Management (TTM).
  - Obtain brain CT.
  - Start EEG monitoring.
  - Continue comprehensive ICU care.

-------------------------------------------------
TARGETED TEMPERATURE MANAGEMENT (TTM)
-------------------------------------------------
• Indication: Comatose after ROSC.  
• Start as soon as possible.  
• Target 32–36°C for 24 hours (cooling device with feedback loop).  
• Continuous temperature monitoring (esophageal, rectal, or bladder).

-------------------------------------------------
ADDITIONAL CRITICAL CARE MANAGEMENT
-------------------------------------------------
• Maintain:
  - Normoxia (SpO₂ 92–98%).
  - Normocapnia (PaCO₂ 35–45 mmHg).
  - Euglycemia.
• Provide:
  - Continuous/intermittent EEG monitoring. (to detect subclinical seizure if have, otherwise no need)
  - Lung-protective ventilation.

-------------------------------------------------
EVALUATE & TREAT REVERSIBLE CAUSES (H’s & T’s)
-------------------------------------------------
• **H’s**
  - Hypovolemia  
  - Hypoxia  
  - Hydrogen ion (acidosis)  
  - Hypokalemia / Hyperkalemia  
  - Hypothermia  

• **T’s**
  - Tension pneumothorax  
  - Tamponade, cardiac  
  - Toxins  
  - Thrombosis, pulmonary  
  - Thrombosis, coronary  

-------------------------------------------------
ONGOING MANAGEMENT
-------------------------------------------------
• Rapid evaluation and correction of reversible causes.  
• Continue expert consultation and multidisciplinary critical care.

NICE GUIDELINE NG158: RECOMMENDATIONS (PAGES 6 - 30)
ARROW-GUIDED POINT FORMAT
================================================================================

1.1 DIAGNOSIS AND INITIAL MANAGEMENT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Suspected DVT Assessment
-> Presenting with leg swelling/pain -> Assess general medical history + physical examination -> Exclude alternative causes
-> Calculate 2-level DVT Wells Score:
   -> [+1] Active cancer (treatment ongoing, within 6 months, or palliative)
   -> [+1] Paralysis, paresis or recent lower extremity plaster immobilisation
   -> [+1] Bedridden >= 3 days or major surgery within 12 weeks requiring general/regional anaesthesia
   -> [+1] Localised tenderness along distribution of deep venous system
   -> [+1] Entire leg swollen
   -> [+1] Calf swelling >= 3 cm compared to asymptomatic side
   -> [+1] Pitting oedema confined to symptomatic leg
   -> [+1] Collateral superficial veins (non-varicose)
   -> [+1] Previously documented DVT
   -> [-2] Alternative diagnosis at least as likely as DVT

DVT Likely (Wells Score >= 2 Points)
-> Offer proximal leg vein ultrasound scan (result within 4 hours if possible)
-> Scan positive -> Offer or continue anticoagulation treatment (or mechanical intervention if contraindicated)
-> Scan positive + symptomatic iliofemoral DVT -> Consider catheter-directed thrombolytic therapy
-> Scan negative -> Offer quantitative D-dimer test
   -> D-dimer negative -> Stop interim anticoagulation -> Think about alternative diagnoses -> Advise patient on symptoms/seeking help
   -> D-dimer positive -> Stop interim anticoagulation -> Offer repeat proximal ultrasound scan 6 to 8 days later
      -> Repeat scan positive -> Treat DVT
      -> Repeat scan negative -> Stop interim anticoagulation -> Think about alternative diagnoses
-> Ultrasound scan delayed > 4 hours -> Offer quantitative D-dimer test -> Offer interim therapeutic anticoagulation -> Perform scan within 24 hours

DVT Unlikely (Wells Score <= 1 Point)
-> Offer quantitative D-dimer test (result within 4 hours)
-> D-dimer delayed > 4 hours -> Offer interim therapeutic anticoagulation while awaiting result
-> D-dimer negative -> Stop interim anticoagulation -> Think about alternative diagnoses -> Advise patient on symptoms/seeking help
-> D-dimer positive -> Offer proximal leg vein ultrasound scan (within 4 hours, or within 24 hours with interim anticoagulation)
   -> Scan positive -> Treat DVT
   -> Scan negative -> Stop interim anticoagulation -> Think about alternative diagnoses

D-Dimer Testing Guidelines
-> Laboratory facilities not immediately available -> Consider fully quantitative point-of-care D-dimer test
-> Age > 50 years -> Consider age-adjusted D-dimer test threshold

Suspected PE Assessment
-> Presenting with chest pain / dyspnoea / haemoptysis -> Assess history + physical exam + Chest X-ray to exclude other causes
-> Low clinical suspicion + alternative diagnosis feasible -> Consider PERC rule (PERC not validated in COVID-19)
-> Calculate 2-level PE Wells Score:
   -> [+3.0] Clinical signs and symptoms of DVT (leg swelling + pain on deep vein palpation)
   -> [+3.0] Alternative diagnosis less likely than PE
   -> [+1.5] Heart rate > 100 beats per minute
   -> [+1.5] Immobilisation >= 3 days or surgery in previous 4 weeks
   -> [+1.5] Previous DVT/PE
   -> [+1.0] Haemoptysis
   -> [+1.0] Malignancy (active, treated in last 6 months, or palliative)

PE Likely (Wells Score > 4 Points)
-> Offer immediate CTPA (or V/Q SPECT / planar scan if contrast media allergy, severe renal impairment CrCl < 30 ml/min, or high radiation risk)
-> Scan delayed -> Offer interim therapeutic anticoagulation while awaiting scan
-> Scan positive -> Offer or continue anticoagulation treatment (or mechanical intervention if contraindicated)
-> Scan positive + Haemodynamic instability -> Offer continuous UFH infusion + Consider thrombolytic therapy
-> Scan negative -> Assess for DVT
   -> DVT suspected -> Consider proximal leg vein ultrasound scan
   -> DVT not suspected -> Stop interim anticoagulation -> Think about alternative diagnoses -> Advise patient

PE Unlikely (Wells Score <= 4 Points)
-> Offer quantitative D-dimer test (result within 4 hours)
-> D-dimer delayed > 4 hours -> Offer interim therapeutic anticoagulation while awaiting result
-> D-dimer positive -> Proceed to immediate CTPA pathway (or V/Q scan)
-> D-dimer negative -> Stop interim anticoagulation -> Think about alternative diagnoses -> Advise patient

Signs of Both DVT and PE
-> Carry out initial diagnostic investigations for either DVT or PE based on clinical judgement


1.2 OUTPATIENT TREATMENT FOR LOW-RISK PE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
-> Low-risk PE suspected or confirmed -> Consider outpatient treatment using a validated risk stratification tool
-> Follow initial diagnostic (1.1) and treatment (1.3) pathways
-> Agree monitoring and follow-up plan -> Provide written symptom/complication info -> Provide direct contact details for thrombosis team -> Provide out-of-hours service details


1.3 ANTICOAGULATION TREATMENT FOR SUSPECTED OR CONFIRMED DVT OR PE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Interim Therapeutic Anticoagulation
-> Suspected proximal DVT or PE with diagnostic delay > 4 hours -> Offer interim therapeutic anticoagulation
-> Drug selection -> Choose an anticoagulant that can be continued if VTE is confirmed
-> Baseline tests -> Measure FBC, renal/hepatic function, PT, APTT -> Start treatment BEFORE results available -> Review/act on results within 24 hours

Confirmed DVT or PE Treatment (Minimum 3 Months)
-> First-line choice -> Offer Apixaban OR Rivaroxaban
-> Second-line (if Apixaban and Rivaroxaban unsuitable) -> Offer ONE of:
   -> LMWH for at least 5 days followed by Dabigatran or Edoxaban
   -> LMWH concurrently with VKA for at least 5 days (or until INR >= 2.0 on 2 consecutive readings), followed by VKA alone
-> Unfractionated Heparin (UFH) + VKA -> Do NOT routinely offer unless patient has renal impairment/established renal failure or increased bleeding risk
-> INR Monitoring -> Do NOT routinely offer self-management or self-monitoring for VKA treatment

Special Populations
-> Extremes of Body Weight (< 50 kg or > 120 kg) -> Consider anticoagulation with regular monitoring of therapeutic levels
-> PE with Haemodynamic Instability -> Offer continuous UFH infusion + Consider thrombolytic therapy
-> Renal Impairment (CrCl 15 to 50 ml/min) -> Offer Apixaban OR Rivaroxaban OR LMWH (>= 5 days) followed by Edoxaban/Dabigatran (if CrCl >= 30 ml/min) OR LMWH/UFH + VKA (until INR >= 2.0) followed by VKA
-> Established Renal Failure (CrCl < 15 ml/min) -> Offer LMWH OR UFH OR LMWH/UFH + VKA (until INR >= 2.0) followed by VKA
-> Active Cancer -> Offer anticoagulation for 3 to 6 months -> Consider DOAC -> If DOAC unsuitable: consider LMWH alone OR LMWH + VKA
-> Cancer in Remission -> Follow standard confirmed DVT/PE treatment recommendations
-> Triple Positive Antiphospholipid Syndrome -> Offer LMWH + VKA for at least 5 days (or until INR >= 2.0 on 2 consecutive readings), followed by VKA alone

Treatment Failure
-> Anticoagulation treatment fails -> Check treatment adherence -> Address other sources of hypercoagulability -> Increase anticoagulant dose OR change to anticoagulant with a different mode of action


1.4 LONG-TERM ANTICOAGULATION FOR SECONDARY PREVENTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Review at 3 Months (3 to 6 Months for Active Cancer)
-> Assess benefits/risks of continuing, stopping, or changing anticoagulant
-> Provoked DVT/PE (provoking factor no longer present + uncomplicated course) -> Consider stopping -> Advise on recurrence risk + provide written info + direct contacts
-> Unprovoked DVT/PE -> Consider continuing beyond 3 months -> Low bleeding risk -> Explain benefits of continuing likely outweigh risks
-> Risk assessment tools -> Do NOT rely solely on predictive risk tools -> Consider HAS-BLED score for bleeding risk (score >= 4 -> discuss stopping)
-> Selecting Anticoagulant:
   -> Standard population -> Continue current anticoagulant if well-tolerated OR consider switching to Apixaban (if current DOAC not well-tolerated/preferences changed)
   -> Special populations (renal/cancer/antiphospholipid/extreme body weight) -> Consider continuing current treatment if well-tolerated
-> Patient declines long-term anticoagulation -> Consider Aspirin 75 mg or 150 mg daily
-> Follow-up -> Review general health, VTE recurrence risk, bleeding risk, and preferences at least ONCE A YEAR


1.5 INFORMATION AND SUPPORT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
-> Provide verbal and written info -> Usage, duration, side effects, interactions (food/drugs/alcohol), monitoring, dental care, pregnancy, activities/travel, seeking help
-> Provide treatment-specific Anticoagulant Alert Card -> Advise carrying at all times
-> Dietary/Ethical considerations -> Note animal origin of Heparins and lactose content in Apixaban/Rivaroxaban


1.6 THROMBOLYTIC THERAPY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
-> Symptomatic Iliofemoral DVT (< 14 days duration + good functional status + life expectancy >= 1 year + low bleeding risk) -> Consider catheter-directed thrombolytic therapy
-> PE with Haemodynamic Instability -> Consider pharmacological systemic thrombolytic therapy
-> PE with Haemodynamic Stability -> Do NOT offer pharmacological systemic thrombolytic therapy


1.7 MECHANICAL INTERVENTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Inferior Vena Caval (IVC) Filters
-> Do NOT offer IVC filter routinely UNLESS part of prospective clinical study OR anticoagulation contraindicated OR PE occurred during anticoagulation
-> Anticoagulation contraindicated -> Consider IVC filter -> Remove filter once anticoagulation is established
-> PE occurred during anticoagulation -> Consider IVC filter ONLY after completing treatment failure steps
-> Requirement -> Document strategy for filter removal before fitting

Elastic Graduated Compression Stockings
-> Do NOT offer to prevent post-thrombotic syndrome or VTE recurrence
-> MAY offer for managing leg symptoms after DVT (explain application, wear duration, replacement)


1.8 INVESTIGATIONS FOR CANCER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
-> Unprovoked DVT/PE -> History review + baseline bloods + physical examination
-> Routine cancer screening -> Do NOT offer further cancer investigations UNLESS relevant clinical symptoms or signs are present


1.9 THROMBOPHILIA TESTING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
-> Continuing anticoagulation -> Do NOT offer testing for hereditary thrombophilia
-> Provoked DVT/PE -> Do NOT offer thrombophilia testing
-> Unprovoked DVT/PE (stopping anticoagulation planned) -> Consider testing for antiphospholipid antibodies OR hereditary thrombophilia (if 1st-degree relative had DVT/PE)
-> First-degree relatives -> Do NOT routinely offer thrombophilia testing to relatives of people with DVT/PE and thrombophilia


TERMS USED IN THIS GUIDELINE (PAGES 28 - 29)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
-> Active Cancer -> Receiving active antimitotic treatment, diagnosed past 6 months, recurrent, metastatic, or inoperable (excludes non-melanoma skin cancer)
-> Provoked DVT/PE -> DVT/PE with recent (< 3 months) transient major clinical risk factor (surgery, trauma, immobility, pregnancy, HRT/COC)
-> Unprovoked DVT/PE -> DVT/PE without recent major clinical risk factor and not on hormonal therapy
-> Proximal DVT -> DVT at or above the level of the popliteal trifurcation area


RECOMMENDATIONS FOR RESEARCH (PAGE 30)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
-> 1. IVC Filters -> Evaluate short- and long-term clinical and cost effectiveness in VTE
-> 2. DOAC Comparisons -> Evaluate comparative clinical/cost effectiveness of DOACs using individual patient data
-> 3. Prediction Tools -> Compare prognostic accuracy of VTE recurrence + bleeding risk tools against clinical judgement
-> 4. Lower-Dose Thrombolysis -> Test efficacy/safety in acute PE with right ventricular dysfunction
-> 5. DVT Diagnosis -> Compare whole-leg vs proximal leg vein ultrasound scanning

31. Infection, sepsis, & inflammation

--- (Melioidosis)

 PATHOPHYSIOLOGY & MANAGEMENT
=====================================================================

1. LATENCY AND REACTIVATION
* Organism can remain dormant in the body, leading to recrudescent (relapsing) disease years after initial exposure.
* Reactivation is typically triggered by physiological stressors or underlying conditions (e.g., diabetes mellitus, immunosuppression, liver disease, concurrent infections, trauma).

2. SEVERE TISSUE DESTRUCTION & ABSCESS FORMATION
* Acute infection can present as fulminant, necrotic, and cavitating pneumonia, rapidly progressing to systemic sepsis and septic shock.
* Frequently seeds throughout the body to form deep abscesses, particularly in the spleen, prostate, and skin.

3. INTRINSIC ANTIBIOTIC RESISTANCE
* Difficult to manage due to variable susceptibility to antimicrobial agents.
* Intrinsic resistance to aminoglycosides removes a primary drug class commonly used for severe Gram-negative infections.

4. PROLONGED THERAPY REQUIREMENTS
* Eradication requires extensive and prolonged antibiotic regimens: minimum 1 month for acute cases, up to 6 months for chronic cases.

MIND MAP: HEAD INJURY & CONTROL OF ICP (PODCAST)
=======================================

→ SCENARIO
   ├─ 24-year-old motorcyclist
   ├─ Profuse bleeding from nose and ears
   ├─ Noisy breathing, reduced GCS
   └─ Trauma team call activated

------------------------------------------------------------
→ IMMEDIATE MANAGEMENT – PRIMARY SURVEY (ABCDE)
------------------------------------------------------------

A. **AIRWAY + CERVICAL SPINE PROTECTION**
   ├─ Airway obstruction likely (noisy breathing, low GCS)
   ├─ Priorities:
   │     • Apply high-flow oxygen
   │     • Perform jaw thrust (avoid head tilt)
   │     • Suction blood and debris from mouth/nose
   │     • Insert oropharyngeal airway if tolerated
   ├─ Call for:
   │     • Senior anaesthetic help
   │     • Anaesthetic assistant
   │     • Prepare for RSI with manual in-line stabilisation (MILS)
   ├─ Considerations:
   │     • Risk of cervical spine injury
   │     • Risk of facial/skull base fractures (CSF leak from nose/ears)
   └─ **Plan:** Definitive airway = tracheal intubation under RSI conditions

------------------------------------------------------------

B. **BREATHING**
   ├─ Assess:
   │     • Respiratory rate
   │     • Oxygen saturation
   │     • Chest movement and symmetry
   │     • Auscultation for air entry
   │
   ├─ Look for:
   │     • Haemothorax
   │     • Pneumothorax
   │     • Flail chest or pulmonary contusion
   │
   ├─ Interventions:
   │     • Decompress tension pneumothorax before applying PPV
   │     • Provide assisted ventilation if inadequate effort
   │
   └─ Goal:
         • Maintain SpO₂ > 94%
         • Avoid hypercarbia and hypoxia (to prevent ↑ICP)

------------------------------------------------------------

C. **CIRCULATION**
   ├─ Assess:
   │     • Central and peripheral pulses
   │     • Capillary refill time and perfusion
   │     • Heart rate and blood pressure
   │
   ├─ Establish IV access:
   │     • Two large-bore cannulae
   │     • Take bloods for:
   │         – FBC
   │         – U&E
   │         – Coagulation screen
   │         – Crossmatch
   │         – ABG
   │         – Thromboelastography (if bleeding/instability)
   │
   ├─ Identify and control bleeding sources
   ├─ Begin balanced resuscitation (blood products if required)
   └─ Avoid hypotension (MAP < 65 mmHg = worsened brain injury)

------------------------------------------------------------

D. **DISABILITY / NEUROLOGICAL ASSESSMENT**
   ├─ Perform **Glasgow Coma Score (GCS):**
   │     • Eye = 2  → to pain
   │     • Verbal = 2  → moans
   │     • Motor = 4  → flexion to pain
   │     • Total = 8 → **severe head injury**
   │
   ├─ Check:
   │     • Pupils (size, equality, reaction)
   │     • Lateralising signs or posturing
   │     • Blood glucose
   │
   └─ Manage as **severe TBI** if GCS ≤ 8 → urgent airway control and neuro referral

------------------------------------------------------------

E. **EXPOSURE / ENVIRONMENT**
   ├─ Fully expose to assess for other injuries
   ├─ Prevent hypothermia
   ├─ Maintain spinal precautions throughout
   └─ Prepare for whole-body trauma CT (head, neck, chest, abdomen, pelvis)

------------------------------------------------------------

→ ADDITIONAL ACTIONS
   ├─ Gather history from paramedics, bystanders, or relatives:
   │     • Allergies
   │     • Medications (anticoagulants)
   │     • Past medical history
   │     • Last meal (for RSI)
   │     • Events leading to trauma (speed, helmet use)
   └─ Early call to neurosurgical centre for potential transfer

------------------------------------------------------------
→ SEVERITY CLASSIFICATION – HEAD INJURY
------------------------------------------------------------
   ├─ **Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS):**
   │     • Eye (E): 1–4  
   │     • Verbal (V): 1–5  
   │     • Motor (M): 1–6  
   │     • Total range = 3–15
   │
   ├─ **Severity categories:**
   │     • Mild: GCS 13–15
   │     • Moderate: GCS 9–12
   │     • Severe: GCS ≤8
   │
   └─ **Management note:**
         • Moderate & severe TBI → manage at specialist neurosurgical centre

------------------------------------------------------------
→ SUMMARY – KEY POINTS
------------------------------------------------------------
   • Follow ABCDE trauma approach, treat life threats early  
   • RSI with MILS for airway protection if GCS ≤8  
   • Avoid hypoxia, hypercapnia, and hypotension  
   • Control bleeding, maintain CPP  
   • Document GCS and pupils for ongoing assessment  
   • Early CT and neurosurgical involvement essential

MIND MAP: INTUBATION IN TRAUMATIC BRAIN INJURY (TBI)
=====================================================

→ PURPOSE
   ├─ Protect airway
   ├─ Optimise oxygenation and ventilation
   ├─ Prevent secondary brain injury (from hypoxia, hypercarbia, hypotension)
   └─ Facilitate safe transfer to neurosurgical centre

------------------------------------------------------------
→ INDICATIONS FOR INTUBATION & VENTILATION (NICE / AoA GUIDELINES)
------------------------------------------------------------

A. **Airway Protection**
   ├─ GCS ≤ 8 → unable to maintain airway reflexes
   ├─ Loss of gag or cough reflex
   ├─ Unstable facial fractures compromising airway
   ├─ Oropharyngeal bleeding or vomiting
   └─ Seizures compromising airway patency

B. **Ventilatory Insufficiency**
   ├─ Hypoxaemia: PaO₂ < 13 kPa (on oxygen)
   ├─ Hypercarbia: PaCO₂ > 6 kPa (hypoventilation)
   ├─ Spontaneous hyperventilation with PaCO₂ < 4 kPa
   │     → risk of cerebral vasoconstriction & ↓ CBF
   └─ Ineffective respiratory effort or irregular pattern

C. **Neurological Deterioration**
   ├─ Fall in total GCS by ≥ 2 points
   ├─ Fall in motor score by ≥ 1 point
   ├─ Evidence of rising ICP or uncal herniation (pupil asymmetry, Cushing’s triad)
   └─ Prior to transfer to neurosurgical centre (especially if long journey)

D. **Other Situations**
   ├─ During emergency procedures (CT scan, surgery)
   ├─ Agitation preventing safe management or imaging
   └─ Multi-system trauma requiring full airway control

------------------------------------------------------------
→ OBJECTIVES OF INTUBATION
------------------------------------------------------------
   • Maintain oxygenation (SpO₂ > 94%, PaO₂ > 13 kPa)
   • Maintain normocapnia (PaCO₂ 4.5–5.0 kPa)
   • Avoid hypotension (SBP < 100 mmHg → worsens outcome)
   • Protect cervical spine at all times
   • Minimise ICP surges during laryngoscopy/intubation

------------------------------------------------------------
→ TECHNIQUE – RAPID SEQUENCE INDUCTION (RSI)
------------------------------------------------------------

A. **Preparation**
   ├─ Use airway checklist
   ├─ Ensure:
   │     • Full monitoring (ECG, SpO₂, NIBP, EtCO₂)
   │     • 2 large-bore IV cannulae
   │     • Airway equipment ready (VL, bougie, suction)
   │     • Emergency airway plan (e.g. surgical airway)
   │
   ├─ Call for:
   │     • Senior anaesthetist
   │     • Skilled assistant
   │     • Manual in-line stabilisation (MILS)
   │
   └─ Preoxygenate:
         • 100% O₂ for ≥ 3 minutes or 8 vital capacity breaths

B. **Induction Plan**
   ├─ **Drugs of choice (neuroprotective RSI):**
   │     • Fentanyl 3–5 µg/kg → blunt sympathetic response
   │     • Ketamine 1–2 mg/kg → maintains CPP & stable haemodynamics
   │     • Rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg → rapid paralysis
   │     • Vasopressors (e.g. metaraminol, phenylephrine) on standby
   │
   ├─ **Alternatives:**
   │     • Etomidate 0.3 mg/kg (stable CV profile)
   │     • Propofol 1–2 mg/kg (use cautiously; may ↓ BP)
   │
   ├─ **Technique:**
   │     • RSI with cricoid pressure (if indicated) + MILS
   │     • Videolaryngoscope preferred for first-pass success
   │     • Avoid multiple attempts (minimise ICP spikes)
   │
   └─ **Confirmation:**
         • Capnography (gold standard)
         • Bilateral chest auscultation & chest rise
         • Secure tube and maintain head neutral

------------------------------------------------------------
→ POST-INTUBATION MANAGEMENT
------------------------------------------------------------
   ├─ Start controlled ventilation:
   │     • Target PaCO₂ 4.5–5.0 kPa
   │     • Avoid hyperventilation unless acute herniation
   │
   ├─ Maintain MAP ≥ 80 mmHg to preserve CPP
   ├─ Continue sedation:
   │     • Propofol + Alfentanil or Remifentanil infusion
   │
   ├─ Apply neuroprotective strategies:
   │     • Head up 30°
   │     • Avoid venous obstruction (neutral neck)
   │     • Control temperature, glucose, and seizures
   │
   ├─ Reassess pupils and GCS post-intubation
   └─ Prepare for CT brain and neurosurgical referral

------------------------------------------------------------
→ SUMMARY CHECKLIST
------------------------------------------------------------
   ✅ Indications:
        • GCS ≤ 8, airway loss, hypoxia, hypercarbia, or deterioration  
   ✅ Goals:
        • Oxygenation, normocapnia, stable BP, protect C-spine  
   ✅ Technique:
        • RSI with MILS using ketamine, fentanyl, rocuronium  
   ✅ Post-intubation:
        • Controlled ventilation, neuroprotection, sedation, CT + transfer


MIND MAP: SPINAL SURGERY AND NEUROMUSCULAR MONITORING
======================================================

→ Scenario
   ├─ 55-year-old patient for thoracic spine fusion
   ├─ Neurophysiologist present for intraoperative neuromonitoring (IONM)

------------------------------------------------------------
→ What is Intraoperative Neuromonitoring (IONM)?
------------------------------------------------------------
   ├─ Definition:
   │   • Use of various modalities to monitor integrity of neural pathways during surgery
   │   • Detects impending neurological injury early
   │   • Allows prompt intervention to prevent permanent deficit

------------------------------------------------------------
→ When is IONM Used?
------------------------------------------------------------
   ├─ Indications: any surgery with risk of neural damage
   ├─ Common procedures:
   │   • Intracranial surgery – tumour or vascular lesion resection
   │   • Spinal surgery – spinal tumour resection, deformity correction, decompression
   │   • Aortic surgery – risk of spinal cord ischaemia
   │   • Surgery near cranial/peripheral nerves – e.g. parotid or thyroid surgery

------------------------------------------------------------
→ Modalities of IONM
------------------------------------------------------------
   ├─ 1. **Spontaneous potentials**
   │     • Electroencephalography (EEG)
   │     • Electromyography (EMG)
   │
   ├─ 2. **Evoked potentials**
   │     • Somatosensory Evoked Potentials (SSEPs)
   │     • Motor Evoked Potentials (MEPs)
   │     • Brainstem Auditory Evoked Potentials (BAEPs)
   │     • Visual Evoked Potentials (VEPs)
   │
   └─ 3. **Stagnara Wake-Up Test**
         • Involves waking patient intraoperatively to test voluntary motor function
         • Ask patient to move feet or limbs
         • Simple but lacks sensitivity for subtle neurological injury
         • Mostly replaced by electrophysiological methods


SOMATOSENSORY & MOTOR EVOKED POTENTIALS (SSEPs & MEPs)
===================================================================

→ Purpose
   ├─ Used in intraoperative neuromonitoring to detect neurological injury
   ├─ Assess integrity of sensory and motor pathways
   └─ Aim: allow early detection → prevent permanent neurological deficit

------------------------------------------------------------
→ SOMATOSENSORY EVOKED POTENTIALS (SSEPs)
------------------------------------------------------------
   ├─ Function:
   │   • Monitor somatosensory pathways (dorsal columns + spinothalamic tracts)
   │   • Detect compromise in sensory transmission from periphery → cortex
   │
   ├─ Technique:
   │   • Peripheral nerve stimulation (posterior tibial, ulnar, or median nerves)
   │   • Sensory cortex monitored via scalp electrodes
   │   • Signal amplitude: very small (1–2 µV vs 100 µV in EEG)
   │   • Multiple stimulations → averaged signal for clarity
   │
   ├─ Interpretation:
   │   • Reduction in amplitude ≥50% → possible neurological compromise
   │   • Increase in latency ≥10% → delayed conduction / injury
   │
   └─ Notes:
       • Indirectly assesses spinal cord and sensory pathways
       • Commonly used in spinal and intracranial surgeries

------------------------------------------------------------
→ MOTOR EVOKED POTENTIALS (MEPs)
------------------------------------------------------------
   ├─ Function:
   │   • Monitor corticospinal (motor) tracts
   │   • Detects compromise of descending motor pathways
   │
   ├─ Technique:
   │   • Stimulation of motor cortex via scalp electrodes (or direct stimulation)
   │   • Record compound muscle action potentials via intramuscular needle electrodes
   │   • Common muscles: adductor hallucis, tibialis anterior, vastus medialis
   │
   ├─ Interpretation:
   │   • Reduction in amplitude ≥50% → significant motor pathway injury
   │
   └─ Comparison to SSEPs:
       • MEPs = more sensitive and faster to detect changes
       • MEPs affected by anaesthetic depth and muscle relaxants
       • SSEPs more stable but slower to show neurological change

------------------------------------------------------------
→ SUMMARY COMPARISON
------------------------------------------------------------
   ├─ SSEPs → Monitor sensory pathways → Peripheral stimulation → Scalp recording
   ├─ MEPs  → Monitor motor pathways → Cortical stimulation → Muscle recording
   └─ Both used together for comprehensive spinal cord monitoring

MIND MAP: ANAESTHETIC & PHYSIOLOGICAL EFFECTS ON SSEPs AND MEPs
=================================================================

→ OVERVIEW
   ├─ Many anaesthetic agents alter amplitude and latency of evoked potentials
   ├─ Always inform the neurophysiologist when administering new drugs
   └─ Goal: preserve reliable signals while maintaining anaesthetic depth and immobility

------------------------------------------------------------
→ VOLATILE ANAESTHETIC AGENTS
------------------------------------------------------------
   ├─ All volatile agents ↓ amplitude (dose-dependent) and ↑ latency of SSEPs & MEPs
   ├─ Desflurane / Sevoflurane:
   │     • May be used up to 1.0 MAC (preferably <0.5–0.8 MAC)
   │     • Beyond this → marked signal suppression
   ├─ Nitrous oxide:
   │     • Significantly ↓ SSEP amplitude → should be avoided
   └─ Isoflurane / Halothane:
         • Profound depression of both MEPs and SSEPs
         • Usually avoided for neuromonitoring cases

------------------------------------------------------------
→ INTRAVENOUS ANAESTHETIC AGENTS
------------------------------------------------------------
   ├─ Propofol:
   │     • ↓ amplitude (dose-dependent), less effect than volatiles
   │     • Preferred agent in TIVA techniques for neuromonitoring
   ├─ Thiopentone:
   │     • Minimal effect on SSEPs and MEPs at anaesthetic doses
   ├─ Ketamine:
   │     • ↑ amplitude of both SSEPs and MEPs
   ├─ Etomidate:
   │     • ↑ amplitude of SSEPs and MEPs
   └─ Technique note:
         • TIVA (Propofol + Remifentanil) = anaesthetic of choice

------------------------------------------------------------
→ MUSCLE RELAXANTS
------------------------------------------------------------
   ├─ Effect on SSEPs:
   │     • Beneficial — reduces artefacts due to movement
   ├─ Effect on MEPs:
   │     • Detrimental — abolishes response dependent on motor pathway integrity
   └─ Practical approach:
         • Use minimal or no NMBAs after intubation (if MEPs monitored)

------------------------------------------------------------
→ OPIOIDS
------------------------------------------------------------
   ├─ Minimal effect on SSEPs and MEPs
   ├─ Remifentanil:
   │     • Excellent adjunct to TIVA → allows lower propofol dose
   │     • Maintains haemodynamic stability
   └─ Fentanyl / Morphine / Alfentanil:
         • Also minimal impact on evoked potentials

------------------------------------------------------------
→ BENZODIAZEPINES
------------------------------------------------------------
   ├─ Minimal depressant effect on SSEPs and MEPs
   └─ Occasionally used for anxiolysis or sedation pre-induction

------------------------------------------------------------
→ PHYSIOLOGICAL VARIABLES AFFECTING SSEPs & MEPs
------------------------------------------------------------
   ├─ General principle:
   │     • Neural tissue highly dependent on oxygen delivery
   │     • Any ↓ in perfusion or O₂ transport impairs signals
   │
   ├─ Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP):
   │     • MAP <60 mmHg → risk of spinal cord ischaemia
   │     • ↓ amplitudes of both SSEPs & MEPs
   │     • MEPs more affected (corticospinal tracts perfused by single anterior spinal artery)
   │
   ├─ Hypoxaemia:
   │     • ↓ amplitude, ↑ latency of SSEPs
   │
   ├─ Hypocapnoea:
   │     • ↓ SSEP amplitude (due to cerebral vasoconstriction)
   │
   ├─ Anaemia:
   │     • Hct <15% → ↓ amplitude, ↑ latency of SSEPs
   │
   ├─ Hypothermia:
   │     • ↓ amplitude of SSEPs & MEPs
   │     • ↑ latency of SSEPs
   │
   └─ Hyperthermia:
         • Mild (<39°C) → ↓ latency
         • Severe (>39°C) → ↑ latency & ↓ amplitude

==============================================================
RESPONSE TO SIGNIFICANT CHANGE IN EVOKED POTENTIALS
==============================================================

→ Detection of Signal Change
   ├─ Neurophysiologist detects alteration in SSEP/MEP amplitude or latency
   ├─ Immediate communication to surgical and anaesthetic teams
   └─ Collaborative action required (multidisciplinary response)

------------------------------------------------------------
→ INITIAL RESPONSE
------------------------------------------------------------
   ├─ Follow Standard Operating Procedure (if available)
   ├─ Surgeon:
   │     • Stop surgical manipulation immediately
   │     • Relieve any compression on neural structures
   │     • Reassess recent surgical steps (e.g. remove or adjust screws, retractors)
   │     • Consider imaging or repositioning if applicable
   │
   ├─ Anaesthetist:
   │     • Check and correct physiological causes:
   │           – Ensure adequate Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP)
   │           – Maintain oxygenation (PaO₂)
   │           – Maintain normocapnia (PaCO₂)
   │           – Ensure normal haemoglobin and temperature
   │     • Check anaesthetic depth (avoid excessive propofol or volatile)
   │     • Confirm appropriate neuromuscular blockade (avoid over-paralysis)
   │
   └─ Neurophysiologist:
         • Check all electrodes, cables, and connections
         • Repeat measurements to exclude artefact
         • Confirm whether signal improves after interventions

------------------------------------------------------------
→ IF NO IMPROVEMENT AFTER INITIAL ACTIONS
------------------------------------------------------------
   ├─ Reconfirm accuracy of neuromonitoring readings
   ├─ Revisit all possible surgical and anaesthetic factors
   ├─ Consult senior or experienced colleagues (surgical and anaesthetic)
   ├─ Consider pharmacological intervention:
   │     • Administer methylprednisolone 30 mg/kg IV (neuroprotective dose)
   │
   └─ Surgical decision-making:
         • Assess risk–benefit of continuing current procedure
         • Consider staging, limiting extent, or aborting surgery if risk persists

STRESS CARDIOMYOPATHY (TAKOTSUBO SYNDROME)
============================================
uptodate 2025

DIAGNOSIS
----------

🔹 **When to suspect**
- Middle-aged or postmenopausal women.
- Presentation mimics **Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS)**:
  - Chest pain, dyspnea.
  - ECG changes ± troponin elevation.
- **Clue:** Clinical + ECG findings **disproportionate** to biomarker elevation.
- Often preceded by **emotional or physical stressor** (but not always).

-------------------------------------------------
MAYO CLINIC DIAGNOSTIC CRITERIA (All 4 required)
-------------------------------------------------

1. **Transient LV systolic dysfunction**
   - Hypokinesis, akinesis, or dyskinesis.
   - Regional wall motion abnormality **extends beyond one coronary distribution**.
   - Variants:
     - Classic (apical ballooning)
     - Mid-ventricular, basal (reverse), focal, or global types.

2. **Absence of obstructive coronary disease or plaque rupture**
   - Coronary angiography: no significant stenosis or acute plaque rupture.
   - Diagnosis still possible if coronary disease present, *but* wall motion abnormality
     is **not in the same coronary territory**.
   - Seen in ~15% of patients with concurrent CAD (Takotsubo registry data).

3. **New ECG abnormalities or modest troponin rise**
   - ECG:
     - ST-segment elevation and/or T-wave inversion.
   - Troponin:
     - Mild to moderate elevation (disproportionately low vs ECG changes).

4. **Exclusion of alternative diagnoses**
   - **No pheochromocytoma**
   - **No myocarditis**

-------------------------------------------------
SUMMARY CHECKLIST
-------------------------------------------------
✅ Transient LV dysfunction (regional, beyond single vessel)
✅ No obstructive CAD / plaque rupture
✅ New ECG changes ± mild troponin rise
✅ No myocarditis or pheochromocytoma

-------------------------------------------------
KEY CLINICAL PEARLS
-------------------------------------------------
- Mimics MI but coronary angiogram often normal.
- Trigger: emotional/physical stress (“broken heart syndrome”).
- Prognosis: usually reversible within weeks.

- EVD drainage

Second tier:
- Metabolic suppression (barbiturate coma)

Emergent Overt AIH

- If ICP >20 despite above → osmotic therapy

Massive/post-op cerebral edema

Evidence:
- Improves survival & outcomes in severe ICP
- Thiopental > Pentobarbital (better ICP control)

🩸 Rhabdomyolysis
=====================
🔎 Diagnosis
Clinical suspicion
-Trauma, crush, immobilization, seizures, toxins, heat stroke, -sepsis
-Muscle pain, swelling, weakness
-Dark “cola-colored” urine

Key labs
-CK ≥5× ULN (~>1,000 U/L) → diagnostic
-Severe: >5,000 U/L
-Urine dipstick: +blood, no RBCs (myoglobinuria)
-↑ AST/LDH, ↑ myoglobin (early)

Electrolytes to monitor
-↑ K⁺ (arrhythmia risk)
-↑ PO₄³⁻
-↓ Ca²⁺ (later rebound ↑)

Complications
-Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)
-Hyperkalemia arrhythmia
-Compartment syndrome
-DIC

Prognosis

McMahon Score ≥6 → high AKI risk

💉 Management
1. Fluids (mainstay)
-Early aggressive IV hydration (ns 0.9% also canp
-Isotonic sodium bicarbonate preferred (using 1.26% up to 500ml/hour)
-Typical: 200–500 mL/hr (titrate to maintain urine output ≥200 mL/hr)
-Avoid potassium-containing fluids (no Ringer’s lactate)
- UFEME: aim urine PH > 6.5

2. Electrolyte control
-Hyperkalemia → IV calcium, insulin+glucose, salbutamol, dialysis if refractory
-Hypocalcemia → Treat only if symptomatic (seizures, arrhythmia)
-Hyperphosphatemia → binders if severe

3. Adjuncts (selected cases)
-Urine alkalinization with bicarbonate if severe CK (>10–20k) and metabolic acidosis
-Mannitol: only if fluid resuscitation alone insufficient (controversial)

4. Renal support
-Monitor creatinine, urine output hourly
-Dialysis if severe AKI, refractory hyperkalemia, fluid overload

5. Compartment syndrome
-Assess limb pressures if tense, swollen compartments
-Urgent surgical fasciotomy if confirmed


# Hypernatremia 

1. History & Physical

**Review inputs/outputs**:
  * Polyuria >3 L/day → DI or osmotic diuresis
  * GI losses (diarrhea, NG aspirates)
  * Inadequate free water intake

**Medications causing hypernatremia**:
  * Amphotericin, Cidofovir, Cisplatin, Foscarnet, Furosemide
  * Hypertonic saline / bicarbonate
  * Ifosfamide, Lactulose, Lithium, Mannitol
  * High-dose K⁺, SGLT2 inhibitors

**Disease states**:
  * Severe hyperglycemia
  * Neurological disease (esp. brain death)

2. Labs – Urine Osmolality (Key Test)

**<300 mOsm** → Complete DI (central or nephrogenic)
**300–500 mOsm** → Partial DI (central or nephrogenic)
**>600 mOsm** → Normal physiologic response (rules out DI)

3. DDAVP Response Test

**Steps**
1. Baseline urine osmolality
2. Give 2 mcg IV DDAVP once
3. Recheck urine output & osmolality after 2 hrs

**Interpretation**

**Complete nephrogenic DI**: No change, stays <300 mOsm
**Partial nephrogenic DI**: No change, \~300–500 mOsm
**Complete central DI**: Baseline <300 → rises >200 mOsm (often >500)
**Partial central DI**: Baseline 200–500 → rises >200 mOsm

Here’s a **Quick Reference Note** for **Hypernatremia – Treatment of Specific Causes** 👇

---

Hypernatremia – Treatment of Specific Causes

🔹 General Principles

* Always treat **underlying disorder**
* Supportive care: replace lost **water + electrolytes**
* Monitor: serum Na⁺, urine output, osmolality, volume status

🔹 Central Diabetes Insipidus (CDI)
**Mainstay**: Replace vasopressin activity
⚠️ Risk: excessive retention → hyponatremia → requires **close monitoring**

**Options**

**DDAVP clamp:**
  * 2 mcg IV q8h → strong effect, rapid correction
  * Once Na⁺ controlled → reduce to 1–2 mcg IV BID (allow some free water clearance)
**Intranasal DDAVP:**
  * 10 mcg BID (range 10–40 mcg/day)
  * Useful for long-term management
**Oral DDAVP:** Variable bioavailability → less reliable
**Vasopressin infusion:** 0.001–0.01 U/min
  * Useful if vasopressor effect also needed
  * Advantage: titratable, can stop quickly if hyponatremia develops

🔹 Nephrogenic Diabetes Insipidus (NDI)

Core principle: **replace free water losses**
**Adjuncts**:

**Thiazides**:
    * Mild hypovolemia → ↑ proximal tubular water reabsorption
    * ↓ urine diluting ability → ↑ urine osmolality
    * ⚠️ Avoid in lithium patients (risk of worsening toxicity)
 **Amiloride**:
    * Enhances natriuresis
    * Limits K⁺ wasting caused by thiazides

Hypernatremia – Sodium Reduction & Free Water Replacement

🔹 Optimal Rate of Sodium Reduction

**Acute hypernatremia (<48h)**

* Correct **rapidly** (brain not adapted, low risk cerebral edema).
* Rapid correction safer than prolonged hypernatremia.
* Severe cases with oliguria → consider **hemodialysis** (avoid fluid overload).

**Chronic hypernatremia (>48h)**

* Brain adapts → risk of cerebral edema if corrected too fast.
* Traditional: ↓ Na⁺ by **≤12 mmol/day (0.5 mmol/L/hr)**.
* Some authors: up to **1 mmol/L/hr** may be safe.
* **>40 yrs, no cerebral edema:** rapid correction **does not seem harmful**; slower correction linked to worse outcomes.
* **<40 yrs (esp. young adults, premenopausal women):** use conservative target (≤12 mmol/day).

---
🔹 Free Water Replacement

**Strategy**

* **Ad lib**: For alert, thirsty patients → give free access to water.
* **Goal-directed**: For comatose/severe patients → calculate and replace.

**Calculation (24h goal)**

1. Calculate free water needed to lower Na⁺ by \~12 mmol in 24h.
2. Add \~1 L for insensible/ongoing losses.
3. If ongoing diarrhea/polyuria → replace additional losses.

   * Polyuria >3 L/day → replace excess 1:1 with free water.

**Administration**

**Enteral route preferred** (via NG/OG tube).
If not possible → **IV D5W (or D3W)**.

**Monitoring**

* Electrolytes q12h (or more frequent in unstable pts).
* Overcorrection is rare, **undercorrection more common** (due to ongoing losses).
* Adjust therapy daily based on sodium trajectory.


Thrombocytopenia in ICU – Common Causes
1️⃣ Pseudothrombocytopenia
Mechanism: EDTA-induced platelet clumping → falsely low count
Clues:
-Clumps on smear ± platelets coating neutrophils (“satellitism”)
-↑ MPV
-Count corrects in citrate tube (“blue top”)
-Usually benign, sometimes linked to lupus, vasculitis, lymphoma

2️⃣ Reduced Platelet Production
Pattern: Subacute/chronic; may have pancytopenia
Causes:
-Infections: EBV, VZV, HIV, parvovirus
-Drugs/toxins: Alcohol, antivirals, linezolid, penicillins/cephs, TMP-SMX, chemo, NSAIDs, thiazides
-Nutritional: B12, folate deficiency (iron deficiency rarely ↓ platelets)
-Bone marrow/cirrhosis: MDS, marrow infiltration, ↓ thrombopoietin

3️⃣ Non-Immune Consumption
Pattern: Gradual drop; transfusion effective
Causes:
-Coagulopathy: DIC, sepsis, trauma, surgery, large PE/DVT
-Devices: IABP, Impella, HD, ECMO
-Infections: Tick-borne (anaplasmosis, babesiosis, RMSF), hantavirus, leptospirosis
-Microangiopathies: TTP, HUS, HELLP, HLH, CAPS

4️⃣ Immune-Mediated Consumption
Pattern: Abrupt fall (>50% in 24–48h), severe, poor transfusion response
Causes:
-Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)
-Drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia (D-ITP)
-Post-transfusion purpura
-Passive alloimmune thrombocytopenia
-ITP (diagnosis of exclusion)

5️⃣ Abnormal Distribution/Dilution
Causes:
-Massive transfusion
-Splenic sequestration
-Hypothermia (splenic/peripheral pooling)

Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI) – Quick Reference
🔹 Classification (GCS after resuscitation)
-Mild: 13–15
-Moderate: 9–13
-Severe: ≤8

🔹 Epidemiology
Leading cause of death/disability in young adults
-Incidence: 1.4 million/year
-Mortality: 6–10/100,000/yr
-80% male
-Minor injuries (89%): Falls 22–43%, Assault 30–50%, RTC 25%
-Moderate–severe (11%): Mostly RTCs

🔹 Pathophysiology
Primary Injury (at impact)
-Mechanism: compression, shearing, contusion, haemorrhage, oedema
-Microscopic: neuronal membrane disruption, BBB disruption, ionic derangement

Secondary Injury (hours–days later)
-Inflammatory & neurotoxic cascade → vasogenic oedema
-BBB disruption, cerebral hyperaemia, impaired autoregulation
-Cerebral ischaemia (focal/global)
-Excess excitatory neurotransmitters (glutamate, aspartate)
-Free radicals, apoptosis
-Exacerbated by systemic insults: hypoxia, hypo/hypercapnia, hypotension, hypo/hyperglycaemia

Acute Management of Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI)
🔹 Pre-Hospital Care

Standard trauma protocols (ATLS)

Correct:

Hypoxia → worsens outcome

Hypotension → even single episodes ↑ morbidity/mortality; duration correlates with mortality

🔹 Initial ED/ICU Management (ABCDE + Neuroprotection)
Airway
-Intubation & ventilation as needed

Respiratory
-SpO₂ >97%
-PaO₂ 10–13 kPa
-PaCO₂ 4.5–5.0 kPa (ETCO₂ 4.0–4.5)

Cardiovascular
-MAP >90 mmHg
-Manage haemorrhage (most common hypotension cause)
-Reverse anticoagulants
-Avoid albumin (↑ mortality, SAFE trial)

Neurological
-Maintain CPP >60 mmHg
-Keep ICP <20 mmHg
-Treat seizures
-Provide sedation + analgesia

Metabolic
-Na⁺ 145–155 mmol/L
-Glucose 6–10 mmol/L
-Avoid hyperthermia

🔹 Imaging – NICE CT Criteria
CT Head if:
-GCS <13 (initial) OR <15 at 2 hrs
-Suspected open/depressed skull fracture
-Basal skull fracture signs (hemotympanum, CSF leak, Battle’s sign, panda eyes)
-Post-traumatic seizure
-Focal neurological deficit
-≥1 vomiting episode post-injury
-LOC/amnesia + (age >65, dangerous mechanism, >30 min retrograde amnesia, bleeding disorder)

Other imaging:
-Trauma CT (brain + C-spine)
-MRI rarely acutely useful
-Skull X-ray only for NAI survey in children

🔹 Transfer
-Resuscitate & stabilise before transfer
-Time-critical transfer → neurosurgical centre

Airway

Gold standard: Intubation & Ventilation (I&V)
-Risks: hypoxia, raised ICP, full stomach, C-spine, maxillofacial trauma

Indications for I&V
-Loss of airway reflexes
-Facial trauma (bilateral mandible, skull base)
-Hypoxaemia or hypercarbia
-Spontaneous hyperventilation (PaCO₂ <4 kPa)
-GCS ≤8
-Motor score ↓ >2
-Seizures
-Process
-AAGBI monitoring
-Emergency intubation checklist
-Modified RSI + MILS
-Opioid to blunt pressor response
-Propofol (or ketamine) + rocuronium

Ongoing ICU Management

Tier 1
-Head up 30°
-Sedation + analgesia
-Neuromuscular blockade
-EVD ± CSF drainage
-Mild hyperventilation (PaCO₂ 4.0–4.5 kPa)

Tier 2
-Osmotherapy (mannitol, hypertonic saline)

Tier 3
-Aggressive hyperventilation
-Therapeutic hypothermia
-Barbiturate coma
-Decompressive craniectomy

Other Supportive
-VTE prophylaxis
-Adequate nutrition (prefer enteral)

RAISED ICP MANAGEMENT

🔹 Airway & C-spine
-Control airway (tape/anchor-fast ETT, not tied)
-Head midline, loosen C-spine collars if ↑ICP
-Sit up 30–45° (not >45°, risk ↓CPP & paradoxical ↑ICP)

🔹 Respiratory
-Ventilation: PaCO₂ 4.5–5.0 kPa
-Oxygenation: PaO₂ >10–13 kPa, SpO₂ >98%
-PEEP ≈5 cmH₂O (avoid ↑ intrathoracic pressure)

🔹 Cardiovascular
-Maintain CPP >60 mmHg (e.g. MAP 80–90 if ICP 20–30)
-Avoid IJV central lines
-Nimodipine (NG) if SAH for vasospasm
-Avoid hypervolaemia
-Severe systemic HTN → clonidine (α-blocker) / labetalol (β-blocker)
-Avoid CCBs (cause cerebral vasodilation → ↑ICP)

🔹 Neurological
-Sedation: propofol preferred (↓CMR, ↓CBF, ↓ICP)
-Thiopentone/etomidate → reserve for refractory ICP
-Ketamine: controversial, may not worsen outcomes
-Avoid volatiles & N₂O (↑CBF, ↑ICP)
-Opioids: blunt ICP surges, ↓stimulus response, analgesia
-Benzodiazepines: 2nd line (midazolam), modest effect on CMRO₂
-NMBA: prevent coughing/straining (not ICP-lowering)
-⚠️ Sux → transient ICP rise
-Seizure prophylaxis: levetiracetam or phenytoin × 7 days

🔹 Osmotherapy
1)Mannitol 20%
-Dose: 0.5–1 g/kg (2.5–5 ml/kg)
-Onset: 5–20 min; peak 20–60 min; duration ≤6 hrs
-Pros: ↓ICP, rheological effects, free radical scavenger
-Cons: diuresis, rebound ↑ICP, hypoNa → hyperNa, infection risk

2)3% Hypertonic Saline
-Dose: 1–2 ml/kg
-Pros: Equal efficacy, ↑MAP/CPP, anti-inflammatory, safer endpoint (Na⁺ 145–155)
-Cons: hypokalaemia, hyperchloraemic acidosis, Na⁺ fluctuations, coagulopathy, volume overload

🔹 Temperature
-Pyrexia → ↑CMRO₂ by 7% per 1°C
-Treat with paracetamol + active cooling
-Therapeutic hypothermia not recommended (Eurotherm 2015, POLAR 2018, BTF 2016)

🔹 Endocrine
-Steroids:
-Useful in vasogenic oedema from tumour/abscess
-❌ No benefit in TBI or ICH

🔹 Other Homeostasis
-Maintain normoglycaemia
-Ensure adequate nutrition (prefer enteral)
-VTE prophylaxis (timed with neurosurgical advice)

Hydrocephalus – Quick Ref
🔹 Definition & Incidence
-Excessive accumulation of CSF in the head
-Congenital paediatric hydrocephalus: 0.5/1,000 live births
-Adult normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH): 5/100,000

🔹 Classification
Communicating vs Non-communicating (obstructive)
Congenital vs Acquired
1)Communicating – Causes
-Congenital: achondroplasia, craniofacial syndromes, Chiari malformation, vein of Galen aneurysm, Dandy–Walker
-Acquired: SAH, IVH, post-infectious, post-inflammatory adhesions, choroid plexus papilloma

2)Non-communicating – Causes
-Congenital: aqueduct of Sylvius stenosis
-Acquired: tumours, cerebellar haemorrhage, external compression

🔹 Communicating Hydrocephalus
-Free flow of CSF to cisterns/spine
-Impaired reabsorption at arachnoid granulations (e.g. post-SAH)
-Rare: CSF overproduction (choroid plexus papilloma)
-↑ intraventricular pressure → cerebral distension/compression
-Drainage via lumbar route possible

🔹 Non-communicating (Obstructive) Hydrocephalus

-Blockage within ventricular system
-Common: aqueduct of Sylvius stenosis (congenital, acquired, tumour compression)
-Causes acute ↑ ICP
-Lumbar drainage unsafe → requires direct ventricular drainage (EVD)

🔹 Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus (NPH)
-Variant of communicating hydrocephalus
-Chronic, insidious:
-Progressive gait disturbance
-Cognitive decline
-Urinary incontinence
-ICP often normal; no classic raised ICP signs

🔹 Other Types
-External hydrocephalus: CSF in basal cisterns
-Ex-vacuo hydrocephalus: ventricular enlargement due to brain atrophy

Marshall CT Classification – Quick Ref
Category 1 – Normal
No visible pathology
No shift, no cisternal compression

Category 2 – Diffuse Injury II
Visible pathology present
No midline shift >5 mm
Cisterns open
May have small lesions:
High/low density
All lesions <25 cc

Category 3 – Diffuse Injury III (Swelling)
Cisterns compressed or absent
Midline shift 0–5 mm
No lesion >25 cc

Category 4 – Diffuse Injury IV (Shift)
Midline shift >5 mm
No lesion >25 cc

Category 5 – Evacuated Mass Lesion
Any lesion surgically evacuated

Category 6 – Non-evacuated Mass Lesion
Lesion >25 cc not surgically evacuated

✅ Key Point:
Categories I–IV = diffuse injuries (graded by cisterns & shift)
V–VI = mass lesions (evacuated vs not evacuated)
Prognosis worsens with compressed cisterns, larger shifts, and non-evacuated lesions.

Hydrocephalus – Quick Ref
🔹 Clinical Features
-Headache (worse in morning, ↑ICP maneuvers: lying flat, coughing, straining)
-Vomiting (classically without nausea)
-Diplopia (CN VI palsy)
-Parinaud’s syndrome (reduced upward gaze)
-Seizures
-↓ Conscious level
-Chronic hydrocephalus: gait disturbance, cognitive decline, urinary incontinence, headaches, ventriculomegaly

🔹 Investigations
1)Standard neuroimaging (CT/MRI; interpretation may be difficult)
2)CSF dynamics testing:
-Intrathecal/intraventricular saline infusion test
-Measures CSF outflow resistance (Rout, mmHg/min)
-Can assess shunt function

🔹 Management
General
-Asymptomatic, chronic, normal-pressure hydrocephalus → may not need intervention
-Drugs largely ineffective

2) External Ventricular Drain (EVD)
Indications:
-Acute hydrocephalus (e.g. SAH, tumour)
-Remove infected shunt hardware
-Intraventricular drug delivery
Technique:
-Into frontal horn of lateral ventricle
-Zeroed at foramen of Monro (~external auditory meatus)
Complications: haemorrhage, catheter displacement, seizures, infection (5–20%)

3)Lumbar Drain
Uses:
-Facilitate intracranial surgery exposure
-Spinal cord protection (e.g. AAA repair)
-Decompress post-op subgaleal collections

4)CSF Shunt
-Diverts CSF (ventricular or lumbar → peritoneum, pleura, or RA)
-Temporary: external
-Permanent: VP/VA/pleural shunt
-Coatings: antibiotic or silver to ↓ infection risk

5)Endoscopic Third Ventriculostomy (ETV)
-Indication: non-communicating hydrocephalus
-Bypasses aqueductal obstruction
-Advantage: avoids shunt complications & dependency

-Multisystem disorders (e.g. cardiac, major spinal defects)

Sodium Disorders in Head Injury – Quick Ref
🔹 Causes
1)Hyponatraemia:
-SIADH
-Cerebral Salt Wasting (CSW)

2)Hypernatraemia:
Central Diabetes Insipidus (DI)

Other contributors:
-Mannitol-induced diuresis
-Hypertonic saline therapy
-Dehydration (↓ oral intake, ↓ GCS)
-Volume overload (transfusion, crystalloids)
-Physiological diuresis (HTN, hypothermia, hyperglycaemia)
-Post-obstructive diuresis
-Drugs (PPIs, AEDs)

🔹 SIADH (Syndrome of Inappropriate ADH Secretion)
-Pathophysiology: Unregulated ADH release despite normal/low osmolality → water retention

Features:
-Hyponatraemia <135 mmol/L
-↓ Serum osmolality (<280 mOsm/kg)
-Urine osmolality > serum osmolality
-Low urine output (differentiates from CSW)
-Normovolaemia / mild hypervolaemia

Management:
-Treat underlying cause
-Fluid restriction
-Furosemide (promotes free water excretion)
-Demeclocycline / ADH receptor antagonists (rare)

Cerebral Salt Wasting (CSW) 

🔹 Pathophysiology
-Renal sodium loss despite normal renal function
-Mechanisms: ↑ ANP/BNP, ↑ sympathetic activity → natriuresis
-Leads to: polyuria + salt loss → hypovolaemia
-Serum ADH ↑ (appropriate to hypovolaemia)

🔹 Features
-Hyponatraemia (sometimes normal Na)
-↓ Serum osmolality (sometimes normal)
-↑ Urine osmolality (salt-rich)
-↑ Urinary sodium
-High urine output
-Hypovolaemia (key differentiator from SIADH)

🔹 Management
-Replace salt + water (0.9% NaCl)
-If refractory: hypertonic saline, fludrocortisone

Diabetes Insipidus (DI)
🔹 Pathophysiology
-Failure of ADH secretion (central DI)
-Resistance to ADH at renal tubules (nephrogenic DI)
-Result: impaired water reabsorption → polyuria, hypernatraemia, hypovolaemia
-Sodium conserved normally

🔹 Causes
Central DI:
-TBI (20–35%), ICH, SAH, pituitary surgery
-Base of skull fracture, hypothalamic oedema, end-stage cerebral oedema

Nephrogenic DI:
-Hereditary
-Drugs (lithium)
-Electrolytes (hyperCa²⁺, hypoK⁺)
-Alcohol intoxication

🔹 Risk Factors in TBI
-Severe TBI
-Base of skull fracture
-Cerebral/hypothalamic oedema

🔹 Diagnosis
-Urine output >1,000 mL/4h (>250 mL/hr) OR >3,000 mL/24h
-↑ Serum Na⁺ >145 mmol/L
-↑ Serum osmolality >305 mOsm/kg
-↓ Urine osmolality <350 mOsm/kg
-↓ Urine specific gravity <1.010

🔹 Management
If Na⁺ >150–155 mmol/L:
-Replace fluid losses (0.9% NaCl; free water correction later)
-DDAVP (desmopressin) 0.5 µg IV PRN

✅ Exam Differentiation Tip:
SIADH: Hyponatraemia + low urine output + euvolaemia
CSW: Hyponatraemia + high urine output + hypovolaemia
DI: Hypernatraemia + high urine output + hypovolaemia

Status Epilepticus – Quick Ref
🔹 Definition
-30 mins continuous seizure activity OR
-Sequential seizures without full recovery of consciousness between events

🔹 Classification
1)Convulsive SE
-Generalised tonic-clonic
-Partial/focal seizures

2)Non-convulsive SE
-EEG evidence of uncontrolled seizures without obvious convulsions
-Causes: absence seizures, complex partial seizures, anaesthetised patients
-Accounts for ~25% of SE

🔹 Pathophysiology
1)Early phase (0–30/60 min)
-↑ CMRO₂
-↑ CBF
-↑ Glucose & lactate
-Sympathetic surge (↑ CO, MAP, HR, CVP)

2)Late phase (>30–60 min, uncontrolled)
-Failure of compensatory mechanisms
-Cerebral autoregulation impaired → hypoxia, hypoglycaemia, oedema, ↑ICP
-Systemic complications:
>>Electrolytes: hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia
>>Metabolic acidosis
>>Rhabdomyolysis
>>DIC
>>Multi-organ failure

🔹 Aetiology
-Any cause of seizures

- Many possible causes; some specific to ICU (e.g. critical illness neuromyopathy).  
- May also represent exacerbation of existing neuromuscular disease.

-------------------------------------------------
COMMON ICU-RELATED CAUSES
-------------------------------------------------
• **Critical illness neuromyopathy**  
  - Generalized weakness ± sensory loss.  
  - Appears on reducing sedation.  

• **New-onset stroke**  
  - Usually unilateral weakness.  
  - Risk ↑ with cerebral hypoperfusion or coagulopathy.  

• **Metabolic myopathies**  
  - Due to low K+, Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, or uraemia.  
  - Correct metabolic abnormalities.  

• **Prolonged effects of muscle relaxants**  
  - Consider low pseudocholinesterase activity or myasthenia gravis (after suxamethonium).  
  - Non-depolarizing agents: test with neostigmine 2.5 mg IV + anticholinergic.  
  - Patients with myasthenia gravis also respond.  

• **Cord compression**  
  - Causes: tumour, trauma, epidural haematoma.  

• **Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS)**  
  - LP: raised CSF protein, normal cell count.  (dissociation of cytoalbumine) 
  - If atypical, repeat LP or do nerve conduction studies (segmental demyelination, slow conduction).  

• **Myasthenia gravis / Eaton–Lambert syndrome**  
  - Fatigable weakness or ptosis → myasthenia gravis.  
  - Confirm with:  
    - IV edrophonium test.  
    - Anti-AChR or anti-MuSK antibody.  
    - Single-fibre EMG.  
  - Eaton–Lambert: proximal leg weakness, sparing ocular muscles.  

• **Other causes**  
  - Identified via history, examination, and specialized testing.

===========================================
GUILLAIN–BARRÉ SYNDROME (GBS) – QUICK REFERENCE
===========================================

-------------------------------------------------
OVERVIEW
-------------------------------------------------
• Immune-mediated acute demyelinating polyradiculopathy.  
• Common triggers: Campylobacter gastroenteritis, viral infections, immunizations.  

-------------------------------------------------
CLINICAL FEATURES
-------------------------------------------------
• Areflexic motor weakness — symmetrical, ascending, may involve cranial nerves (facial, bulbar, extraocular).  
• Minor sensory symptoms (e.g. paraesthesiae).  
• Autonomic dysfunction common (hypo/hypertension, arrhythmias, ileus, urinary retention).  
• CSF: normal cell count, rising protein (>0.4 g/L).  
• Nerve conduction: slow velocities, prolonged F-waves.  
• Muscle tenderness and back pain possible.  
• Main causes of morbidity/mortality: respiratory weakness and autonomic instability.  

-------------------------------------------------
SPECIFIC TREATMENT
-------------------------------------------------
• IVIg: 0.4 g/kg/day × 5 days (effective if started ≤14 days).  
• Plasma exchange: five 50 mL/kg exchanges over 8–13 days.  
• Corticosteroids: not beneficial.  

-------------------------------------------------
SUPPORTIVE TREATMENT
-------------------------------------------------

**Respiratory Care**
  • Regular chest physiotherapy.  
  • Consider mechanical ventilation if patient tiring or PaCO₂ rising.  
  • FVC <15 mL/kg → likely need for ventilation.  
  • Early tracheostomy if prolonged ventilation expected.  
  • Tracheostomy for bulbar involvement or poor cough even if breathing spontaneously.

**Cardiovascular Care**
  • Continuous monitoring for autonomic effects.  
  • Arrhythmias possible, especially with anaesthesia (avoid suxamethonium).  
  • Treat arrhythmias cautiously — exaggerated BP response to vasoactive drugs.  

**Nutritional Support**
  • Enteral nutrition via PEG for long-term cases.  
  • Parenteral nutrition if ileus persists.  

**Analgesia and Anxiolysis**
  • Pain management: NSAIDs or opiates for muscle/abdominal/neuropathic pain.  
  • Benzodiazepines for anxiety or emotional lability.  
  • Monitor mood for depression.  

**Other Support**
  • Multidisciplinary team: physiotherapy, dietitian, speech and language, psychology.  
  • Rehabilitation improves long-term recovery.  
  • Attention to pressure area care and DVT prophylaxis.

LVOT VTI – QUICK REFERENCE (CRITICAL CARE ECHO youtube
===========================================

• LVOT VTI = Left Ventricular Outflow Tract Velocity Time Integral.  
• Represents the length of a column of blood ejected from LV during systole (“stroke distance”).  
• Proportional to stroke volume and cardiac output.  

• Measured using apical 5-chamber view.  
• Start with apical 4-chamber view, tilt probe tail down to visualize LVOT.  
• Activate pulse-wave Doppler and place sample gate at LVOT base near aortic leaflets.  
• Beam should be as parallel as possible to LVOT blood flow.  
• Trace the Doppler waveform to get the area under the curve (the “integral”).  
• Some machines have automated VTI tracing and AI sample gate placement.  

• Normal LVOT VTI range: 18–22 cm.  

• Low VTI (<18 cm): cardiogenic, obstructive, or hypovolemic shock.  
• Normal/high VTI (≥18 cm): distributive (e.g. early septic) shock.  

• Primary value lies in trending measurements (before and after interventions).  
• If LVOT VTI increases by ≥15% after an intervention → cardiac output has improved.  
• Interventions include fluids, diuretics, vasopressors, ventilator or ECMO adjustments.  

• Can combine with passive leg raise (PLR) to test fluid responsiveness.  
• PLR acts as a transient, reversible fluid challenge.  
• Measure baseline LVOT VTI, perform PLR, wait 1 minute, remeasure.  
• ≥15% increase = fluid responsive.  

• Blood pressure change during PLR is unreliable for assessing response.  
• LVOT VTI trending correlates best with thermodilution (gold standard).  
• More accurate than CVP or other noninvasive methods.  

• Works in intubated patients, any tidal volume, and arrhythmias.  

• Limitations:  
  - Poor apical 5-chamber view.  
  - Dynamic LVOT obstruction or severe aortic regurgitation (falsely elevated VTI).  
  - Requires multiple keystrokes and practice.  

• Alternative sites if LVOT not measurable:  
  - Right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT).  
  - Mitral valve inflow.  
  - Based on conservation of mass principle (equal flow through valves if no shunt/regurgitation).  

• Summary: LVOT VTI = noninvasive, quantitative, and trendable indicator of stroke volume and cardiac output; ideal for assessing fluid responsiveness in critically ill patients.

====================================================================
LEFT VENTRICULAR OUTFLOW TRACT OBSTRUCTION (LVOTO) – QUICK REFERENCE
https://youtu.be/2LF3CnkoGQA?si=Jk373MhN7BNyIhmY
====================================================================

• Commonly seen in ICU/ED during septic shock, not just in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM).  
• May cause vasopressor-refractory shock (worsens with norepinephrine).  

-------------------------------------------------
PATHOPHYSIOLOGY
-------------------------------------------------
• LVOT = outflow tract in front of anterior mitral leaflet where LV ejects blood to aorta.  
• LVOTO occurs when anterior mitral leaflet is sucked toward septum during systole (SAM = systolic anterior motion).  
• Caused by Venturi/Bernoulli effect: narrow tract → faster blood flow → lower pressure → suction of leaflet toward septum.  
• Leads to two main problems:  
  - Obstruction of LV outflow → ↓ cardiac output.  
  - Mitral regurgitation → further ↓ forward flow.  
• Dynamic condition (not fixed like aortic stenosis).  
• Worsens with inotropes or decreased LV volume.  

-------------------------------------------------
PRECIPITATING FACTORS
-------------------------------------------------
1. **Hypovolemia** → smaller LV cavity → higher velocity → more SAM.  
2. **Tachycardia** → ↓ diastolic filling → smaller LV cavity → more SAM.  
3. **Inotropes** → ↑ contractility/speed of flow → ↑ suction → more SAM.  

-------------------------------------------------
PREDISPOSING ANATOMICAL FACTOR
-------------------------------------------------
• Basal septal hypertrophy (“sigmoid septum”, “septal bulge”, “septal knuckle”).  
• Prevalence: <60 yrs = 0.1%; >85 yrs = 18%.  
• Common in elderly hypertensive females.  

-------------------------------------------------
ECHO FINDINGS
-------------------------------------------------
• Apical 5-chamber or apical 3-chamber view with **continuous-wave Doppler** across LVOT/aortic valve.  
• Classic **dagger-shaped** Doppler waveform with **late peaking high velocity** (>2.7 m/s).  
• Compare: Aortic stenosis = parabolic, fixed obstruction.  
• May see SAM on parasternal long-axis or apical 3-chamber view (mitral leaflet touching septum).  
• Associated mitral regurgitation jet (posteriorly directed).  
• Structural features: basal septal hypertrophy, Takotsubo with basal hyperkinesis, or thick septum (HCM).  

-------------------------------------------------
DIAGNOSIS – 3 STEPS
-------------------------------------------------
1. Parasternal long-axis: identify basal septal hypertrophy or Takotsubo/HCM features.  
2. Look for SAM (mitral leaflet touching septum in systole, use M-mode if needed).  
3. Apical 5- or 3-chamber: dagger-shaped CW Doppler with velocity >2.7 m/s.  

-------------------------------------------------
CLINICAL PRESENTATIONS
-------------------------------------------------
1. **Elderly septic shock patient** worsening on norepinephrine.  
2. **Cardiogenic shock** (esp. anterior MI) worsening with diuretics/inotropes.  
3. **Takotsubo cardiomyopathy** with hypotension (25% have LVOTO).  
4. **Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy** with critical illness.  

-------------------------------------------------
TREATMENT – “TWO F’s” APPROACH
-------------------------------------------------
1. **Fluids** – expand LV cavity, ↑ separation of septum and mitral valve.  
2. **Phenylephrine** – pure alpha-agonist, ↑ afterload without ↑ inotropy, causes reflex bradycardia → ↑ diastolic filling.  
   - Alternative: Vasopressin (less evidence).  
3. **Stop inotropes** (norepinephrine, epinephrine, dobutamine) – they worsen obstruction.  
4. **Avoid diuretics** – reduce preload and worsen obstruction.  
5. **Consider esmolol** (short-acting beta-blocker) if diagnosis confirmed and severe (velocity >3.5 m/s) → ↓ HR and contractility.  
6. **Avoid intra-aortic balloon pump** in LVOTO-related cardiogenic shock.  

-------------------------------------------------
PHYSIOLOGIC RATIONALE
-------------------------------------------------
• Fluids: ↑ LV size → ↓ velocity → ↓ Venturi suction → less SAM.  
• Phenylephrine: ↑ afterload, ↓ HR, improves LV filling.  
• Beta-blockade: ↓ contractility and HR → ↓ LVOTO gradient.  

-------------------------------------------------
FOLLOW-UP
-------------------------------------------------
• Repeat echo after therapy → confirm ↓ LVOT velocity and resolution of dagger shape.  
• Assess improvement in MR jet.  
• Expect ↑ BP and better hemodynamics with successful treatment.  

-------------------------------------------------
KEY VALUES
-------------------------------------------------
• Normal LVOT velocity ≈ 1 m/s.  
• Abnormal LVOT obstruction > 2.7 m/s.  
• Severe LVOTO ≈ 5 m/s or more.  

-------------------------------------------------
SUMMARY
-------------------------------------------------
• LVOTO = dynamic obstruction caused by SAM of mitral leaflet.  
• Triggered by hypovolemia, tachycardia, inotropes.  
• Common in elderly hypertensive females, Takotsubo, or HCM.  
• Worsens with norepinephrine, improves with fluids + phenylephrine.  
• Diagnostic hallmark: dagger-shaped Doppler tracing with high velocity.  
• Treatment goal: expand LV, reduce contractility, slow heart rate.

INFECTIVE ENDOCARDITIS (IE) – QUICK REFERENCE (UpToDate)
===========================================

- Diagnosis based on clinical findings, blood cultures or microbiologic data, and cardiac imaging.  
- Requires identification of infecting organism (culture, serology, or molecular test) and evidence of cardiac involvement (vegetation, abscess, or structural complication).  
- 2023 Duke–ISCVID criteria used; similar to 2023 ESC Guidelines.

-------------------------------------------------
DEFINITE IE
-------------------------------------------------
- Established by pathologic or clinical criteria.

Pathologic:
  • Microorganisms identified in vegetation, cardiac tissue, prosthetic valve, graft, device, or embolus.  
  • Histopathologic evidence of active endocarditis in same locations.

Clinical:
  • Two major criteria  
  • One major + three minor criteria  
  • Five minor criteria

-------------------------------------------------
POSSIBLE IE
-------------------------------------------------
- One major + one minor criterion  
- Three minor criteria

-------------------------------------------------
REJECTED IE
-------------------------------------------------
- Alternate diagnosis explaining findings.  
- No recurrence after ≤4 days antibiotics.  
- No pathologic evidence after ≤4 days antibiotics.  
- Does not meet clinical criteria for possible or definite IE.

-------------------------------------------------
MAJOR CLINICAL CRITERIA
-------------------------------------------------
Microbiologic:
  • Typical IE-causing organisms in ≥2 blood cultures.  
  • Uncommon organisms in ≥3 blood cultures.  
  • Positive PCR for Coxiella, Bartonella, or Tropheryma whipplei.  
  • Coxiella burnetii IgG >1:800 or isolated from single culture.  
  • Bartonella IgG ≥1:800 (IgM or IgG by immunofluorescence).

Imaging:
  • Echo/CT showing vegetation, perforation, aneurysm, abscess, pseudoaneurysm, or fistula.  
  • New significant valvular regurgitation.  
  • Partial dehiscence of prosthetic valve.  
  • Abnormal metabolic activity on [18F]-FDG PET/CT in native/prosthetic valve (≥3 months post-implant), aortic graft, or intracardiac device.

Surgical:
  • Direct intraoperative evidence of IE without meeting microbiologic or imaging major criteria.

-------------------------------------------------
MINOR CLINICAL CRITERIA
-------------------------------------------------
Predisposition:
  • Previous IE, prosthetic valve, valve repair, congenital heart disease, significant valve disease, CIED, HOCM, injection drug use.

Fever:
  • Temperature ≥38°C.

Vascular phenomena:
  • Arterial emboli, septic pulmonary infarcts, cerebral/splenic abscess, mycotic aneurysm, intracranial hemorrhage, conjunctival hemorrhages, Janeway lesions, purulent purpura.

Immunologic phenomena:
  • Rheumatoid factor, Osler nodes, Roth spots, immune complex glomerulonephritis.

Microbiologic evidence (not meeting major):
  • Positive blood culture not fulfilling major criteria.  
  • Positive culture or PCR for IE-consistent organism from sterile site (non-cardiac).  
  • Single PCR detection of skin bacteria on valve/wire without supporting evidence.

Imaging:
  • [18F]-FDG PET/CT abnormal activity within 3 months of implantation of prosthetic valve, graft, or intracardiac device.

Physical exam (if echo unavailable):
  • New valvular regurgitation by auscultation (not merely worsening murmur).

###################
ATRIAL FIBRILLATION (AF)
###################

DEFINITION
- AF is a supraventricular tachyarrhythmia
- Caused by uncoordinated atrial electrical activity → ineffective atrial contraction

ECG FEATURES
- Absence of P-waves
- Characteristic “irregularly irregular” R-R intervals
- May occasionally have regular R-R intervals if there is concomitant AV-nodal block

DIAGNOSIS
- Requires rhythm documentation by ECG
- Episode of AF must last ≥30 seconds (by convention)

------------------------------------------------------------
CLASSIFICATION  (ESC guideline-based)
- Can be symptomatic or asymptomatic
1. First-diagnosed AF
   • AF not previously diagnosed, regardless of duration or symptom severity
2. Paroxysmal AF
   • Terminates spontaneously or with intervention within 7 days of onset
3. Persistent AF
   • Sustained beyond 7 days, including episodes terminated by drugs/electrical cardioversion after ≥7 days
4. Long-standing persistent AF
   • Continuous AF >12 months’ duration when rhythm-control strategy is adopted
5. Permanent AF
   • No further attempts made to restore/maintain sinus rhythm

------------------------------------------------------------
EPIDEMIOLOGY
- AF is the commonest arrhythmia
- Overall prevalence ≈2%
   • Increases with age: ~5% at 65 yrs, ~10% at 75 yrs
- ≈1.5× more common in men
- More common in Caucasians

PERIOPERATIVE / CRITICAL-CARE AF
- AF often occurs in theatre, post-operative, and critical-care patients
- Incidence higher in these groups than in the general population
- Peak incidence: 2 – 4 days post-operatively

------------------------------------------------------------
PREVALENCE OF POST-OPERATIVE AF  (by clinical setting)
- Vascular / major colorectal surgery:       5 – 10%
- Non-cardiac thoracic surgery:             10 – 30%
- Cardiac surgery:                           25 – 50%
- Critical-care (all-comers):                 4 – 15%
- Critical-care (septic shock):               Up to 40%

------------------------------------------------------------
PATHOPHYSIOLOGY
- Multifactorial mechanisms:
   1. Enhanced automaticity in “sleeves” of atrial tissue (pulmonary veins/vena caval junctions)
      • Acts as trigger points for ectopics → AF
      • Ablation of these foci may provide permanent cure
   2. Multiple re-entering, randomly circulating wavelets
      • Chaotic atrial state → maintains dysrhythmia
   3. Electrical/structural remodelling of atrial tissue
      • Shortens refractory periods → treatment-resistant state
      • Converts paroxysmal AF → persistent AF (~15%/yr)

- Haemodynamic sequelae:
   • Loss of atrial systole (“atrial kick”) → ↓ LV filling → ↓ cardiac output
   • ↓ Diastolic filling → ↑ ventricular rate
   • Tachycardiomyopathy (global cardiomyopathy from rapid rate)
   • Atrial thrombus formation → systemic embolism risk

------------------------------------------------------------
AETIOLOGY
- “Lone AF” no longer recognised; all cases have underlying cause(s)
- ICU/theatre: often due to physiological disturbance (shock, sepsis, electrolytes, hypoxaemia, catecholamines)

CARDIOVASCULAR CAUSES
- Post-MI
- Hypertension (esp. LVH)
- Valvular heart disease (esp. mitral)
- Myocarditis, pericarditis, endocarditis
- Congenital heart disease (e.g. ASD)
- Sick sinus syndrome
- Cardiac surgery
- Atrial myxoma

NON-CARDIAC CAUSES
- Diabetes mellitus
- Acute illness e.g. sepsis
- Catecholamine surges (stress, surgery, phaechromocytoma, exogenous infusions, SAH)
- Pulmonary embolism
- Pulmonary HTN (esp. OSA)
- Alcohol (binges)
- Hyperthyroidism
- Electrolyte disturbances (esp. hypokalaemia)
- Stimulants (caffeine, nicotine)

------------------------------------------------------------
CLINICAL FEATURES
Symptoms:
- Palpitations
- (Pre)-syncope
- Dyspnoea
- Chest tightness/pain
- Fatigue, lethargy
- Reduced exercise tolerance

Signs:
- Myocardial ischaemia
- Tachycardia (120–180 bpm)
- Hypotension
- Acute cardiac failure, pulmonary oedema
- Cardiogenic shock
- Loss of “a” wave and “x” descent on JVP

------------------------------------------------------------
ASSOCIATED OUTCOMES
- Stroke → AF responsible for 20–30% of ischaemic strokes
- LV dysfunction/failure → 20–30% of AF patients
- Cognitive decline (HR 1.4), Vascular dementia (HR 1.6)
- Depression (16–20%)
- Post-op complications → ↑ infection, bleeding, renal impairment
- ↓ Quality of Life (QoL impaired in >60% of patients)
- Hospitalisation → 10–40%/yr, ↑ length of stay, ↑ costs
- Mortality → 1.5–3.5× baseline risk

------------------------------------------------------------
MANAGEMENT OF AF (4 main aims)
1. Restoring sinus rhythm
2. Maintaining sinus rhythm
3. Restoring an acceptable heart rate
4. Reducing thromboembolic risk

------------------------------------------------------------
MANAGEMENT OF ATRIAL FIBRILLATION (AF)
------------------------------------------------------------
STEP 1: CARDIOVERT TO SINUS RHYTHM
- Follow Resus Council tachycardia algorithm
- Amiodarone → ~80% cardioversion success rate
- Class III anti-arrhythmics (“kalants”) such as Vernakalant, Nifekalant, Buddilide
     • Target atrial ion channels → less generalised cardiac effects
     • Vernakalant (IV) rapidly cardioverts AF to sinus rhythm
     • Licensed by NICE
- Seek expert input if using non-standard agents
- Consider DCCV (direct current cardioversion) if haemodynamic instability
     • Success rate in critical care 48–100%
     • Recurrence rate ≈15% post-DCCV

------------------------------------------------------------
STEPS 2 & 3: MAINTENANCE THERAPY & RATE CONTROL
- Major trials (AFFIRM, RACE, PIAF, STAF) → rate control generally preferred over rhythm control
- Rhythm control less favoured for long-term due to atrial remodelling relapse
- Agent options:
     • Class 1C sodium channel blockers
     • Beta-blockers
     • Digoxin
     • Non-DHP calcium channel blockers (e.g. diltiazem)
     • Amiodarone (effective but less suitable long-term due to extracardiac toxicity)
- ESC guideline rate target → HR <100 bpm for post-operative AF
- Choice of agent in peri-operative/critical care similar:
     • Amiodarone often first-line in ICU
     • Magnesium or diltiazem may help
     • Beta-blockers: esmolol, metoprolol
     • Others: flecainide (less used in critical care)

------------------------------------------------------------
STEP 4: ANTICOAGULATION  (Thromboembolism Prevention)
- Anticoagulate all AF patients with CHA₂DS₂-VASc score ≥2
- Offer anticoagulation to men with CHA₂DS₂-VASc ≥1
- Use ORBIT or HAS-BLED scores to assess bleeding risk

First-line agents (DOACs):
- Apixaban 5 mg BD  (or 2.5 mg BD if criteria met)
- Edoxaban 60 mg OD  (or 30 mg OD if <61 kg)
- Rivaroxaban 20 mg OD
- Dabigatran 110–150 mg BD (depends on age)

Second-line:
- Vitamin K antagonist (warfarin) → target INR 2–3

KEY POINTS – PERIOPERATIVE / CRITICAL CARE
- Cardiovert if haemodynamic compromise or instability
- Prefer rate control otherwise
- Amiodarone often most used in ICU
- Continue oxygenation, correct underlying triggers (sepsis, electrolytes, etc.)

DIGIFAB (DIGOXIN-SPECIFIC FAB FRAGMENTS)
- Monovalent IgG Fab binds digoxin (affinity 100–1000× > Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase).

- Refractory hyperkalaemia.

- Avoid unnecessary repeat dosing (risk of hypersensitivity with re-exposure).

- Plasma level ≠ severity; always correlate clinically.
- Correct electrolytes before anti-arrhythmics.
- Atropine or pacing for bradycardia; avoid adrenaline/dopamine.
- Lidocaine for ventricular arrhythmias.

FLOWCHART: AIRWAY PRESSURE RELEASE VENTILATION (APRV) FOR ARDS
================================================================

[Start: Patient with severe ARDS requiring rescue ventilation]
        ↓
──────────────────────────────────────────────
CONCEPT OF APRV
──────────────────────────────────────────────
→ Objective: Maintain high mean airway pressure to maximise alveolar recruitment 
  while allowing intermittent release for CO₂ clearance.

→ Key Principle:
   - "Open the lung, keep it open"
   - Prevent cyclic atelectasis and shear stress
   - Permit spontaneous breathing throughout

→ Basic Mechanism:
   • Constant high pressure (Phigh) maintains alveolar recruitment
   • Brief, intermittent pressure release (Plow) allows ventilation
   • Patient can breathe spontaneously during Phigh

        ↓
──────────────────────────────────────────────
RATIONALE
──────────────────────────────────────────────
→ Standard ARDS ventilation risks:
   - Cyclic collapse/reopening of alveoli → shear injury
   - High driving pressures → VILI

→ APRV aims to:
   - Maintain alveolar stability (autoPEEP)
   - Reduce mechanical power of ventilation
   - Encourage spontaneous effort and early weaning

        ↓
──────────────────────────────────────────────
ADVANTAGES OF APRV
──────────────────────────────────────────────
✓ Maximal alveolar recruitment → reduces shear stress
✓ AutoPEEP → alveoli stay open by design
✓ Lower ventilatory power → less biotrauma
✓ Reduced sedation/paralysis → spontaneous breathing encouraged
✓ Improved diaphragmatic function → better basal aeration
✓ Improved haemodynamics → spontaneous effort improves venous return
✓ Possible improved secretion clearance via high expiratory flow

        ↓
──────────────────────────────────────────────
DISADVANTAGES OF APRV
──────────────────────────────────────────────
✗ Risk of barotrauma (pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum)
✗ Requires higher intravascular volume → conflicts with “dry lung” ARDS strategy
✗ Complex mode → steep learning curve for staff
✗ Risk of right ventricular strain (high mean airway pressures)
✗ Inconsistent tidal volumes (may exceed protective targets)
✗ Potential derecruitment during release phase
✗ Benefits lost if spontaneous breathing suppressed (deep sedation)

        ↓
──────────────────────────────────────────────
PRACTICAL SETTINGS
──────────────────────────────────────────────
→ FOUR MAIN VARIABLES:
   1. **Phigh**  – High pressure (recruitment phase)
   2. **Plow**   – Low pressure (release phase)
   3. **Thigh**  – Time at high pressure
   4. **Tlow**   – Time at low pressure

──────────────────────────────────────────────
SETUP STRATEGY
──────────────────────────────────────────────
1. **Phigh**
   - Set ≈ plateau pressure on conventional mode (typically 28–30 cmH₂O)
   - Determines alveolar recruitment and oxygenation

2. **Plow**
   - Set low (0–5 cmH₂O)
   - Maximises pressure gradient for gas release and CO₂ clearance
   - Adjust upwards if oxygenation poor to increase autoPEEP

3. **Tlow**
   - Short (0.3–0.5 s)
   - Determines autoPEEP and derecruitment risk
   - Adjusted to terminate expiratory flow at ~75% of PEFR
   - Shorter for stiff lungs; longer as compliance improves

4. **Thigh**
   - Typically 9× longer than Tlow (I:E ratio ≈ 9:1)
   - Controls frequency of release breaths and CO₂ clearance
   - Reduced → more releases → ↑ minute volume
   - Increased → fewer releases → weaning transition

        ↓
──────────────────────────────────────────────
WEANING STRATEGY ("DROP AND STRETCH")
──────────────────────────────────────────────
→ Gradually:
   - Decrease Phigh by 2 cmH₂O each step
   - Increase Thigh by 0.5–2 seconds each step
→ Aim:
   - Transition to CPAP with patient breathing spontaneously
   - Minimal pressure releases per minute
   - Extubation once stable on CPAP (~15–20 cmH₂O)

        ↓
──────────────────────────────────────────────
PATIENT SELECTION
──────────────────────────────────────────────
→ Best for:
   - Severe ARDS with refractory hypoxaemia
   - Patients who can breathe spontaneously
→ Avoid in:
   - Severe pulmonary hypertension
   - Right heart failure
   - Contraindications to spontaneous breathing

        ↓
──────────────────────────────────────────────
SUMMARY COMPARISON
──────────────────────────────────────────────
| VARIABLE          | APRV                       | Conventional Ventilation          |
|-------------------|----------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| Pressure pattern  | Continuous high w/ releases| Cyclic inspiration/expiration     |
| Sedation need     | Often minimal              | Often deep                        |
| Spontaneous effort| Encouraged                 | Usually suppressed                |
| Alveolar collapse | Prevented (autoPEEP)       | Frequent                          |
| CO₂ clearance     | Via brief releases         | Via tidal volume                  |

APRV (Airway Pressure Release Ventilation)
Quick Reference Notes
Author: Mastering APRV with Rory Spiegel podcast
Posted: July 24, 2024

-------------------------------------------------
TAKEAWAY LESSONS
-------------------------------------------------
1. **Main Benefit**
   - Restores lungs to FRC (Functional Residual Capacity).
   - Provides higher mean airway pressure vs conventional PEEP.
   - Reduces sedation needs.
   - Automatically titrates “PEEP” based on lung compliance.

-------------------------------------------------
KEY SETTINGS & PHYSIOLOGIC CONCEPTS
-------------------------------------------------
2. **Phigh**
   - Set equal to previous plateau pressure (from conventional mode).
   - Matches higher Phigh to poor compliance.
   - Release volume should be ~normal tidal volume (usually lower due to air trapping).
   - If not lower → Tlow may be too long.

3. **Thigh**
   - Range: 1.5 sec → infinity.
   - Longer = better recruitment; shorter = better ventilation.
   - Initial Thigh ≈ 2 seconds (avoid acidosis from too-long Thigh).
   - Start shorter to match prior minute ventilation; accept slight drop.

4. **Tlow**
   - Terminate when expiratory flow drops to 75% of peak expiratory flow.
   - Typical duration: 0.3–0.6 sec.
   - Prevents alveolar derecruitment.
   - If lungs become more compliant → same Tlow gives 85% termination (acceptable).
   - Avoid lengthening Tlow unnecessarily.

5. **Plow**
   - Usually set to **0 cmH₂O**.
   - Allows fastest exhalation and highest driving pressure.
   - In older vents (Puritan Bennett, Servo) that auto-extend Tlow, Plow may be increased slightly or switch mode.

-------------------------------------------------
RESPONSES DURING RECRUITMENT
-------------------------------------------------
6. As lung recruits:
   - Release volumes gradually increase.
   - Expiratory flow curve flattens.
   - Compliance improves.
   - Larger release volume + low driving pressure = lung protective.

7. Driving pressure on APRV:
   - Can be checked via expiratory hold → measures effective “PEEP”.

-------------------------------------------------
SPONTANEOUS BREATHING
-------------------------------------------------
8. Patients can breathe spontaneously on APRV.
   - Comfort depends on lung recruitment and proximity to FRC.
   - Early phase (unrecruited lungs): avoid spontaneous breathing (use deep sedation/paralysis).
   - Early spontaneous efforts → discomfort + high pressures.
   - Use shorter Thigh + sedation initially.

9. As recruitment improves:
   - Minute ventilation (MV) and PaCO₂ normalize.
   - Sedation can be lightened; spontaneous breathing allowed.
   - APRV patients ultimately need less sedation and paralysis vs conventional modes.

-------------------------------------------------
WEANING STRATEGY
-------------------------------------------------
10. As lungs recruit:
    - CO₂ ↓ gradually, release volume ↑ naturally.
    - When hypocapnia occurs → increase Thigh to reduce ventilation.
    - When MV normal → extend Thigh further → mild hypercapnia.
    - Begin allowing spontaneous breathing.

11. During spontaneous breathing:
    - Comfortable, smooth inspiratory flow = adequate recruitment.
    - If breathing looks labored → lung not yet ready.

12. **Phigh Adjustment During Weaning**
    - Don’t routinely drop Phigh — may cause derecruitment.
    - Adjust Phigh only for disease resolution or signs of overdistention.
    - Use spontaneous breathing as feedback:
        - If lowering Phigh worsens breathing → derecruitment occurred → revert.
    - Spontaneous effort is a faster and more sensitive feedback than release volumes.

-------------------------------------------------
ADDITIONAL PHYSIOLOGIC NOTES
-------------------------------------------------
13. **Permissive Hypercarbia**
    - Acceptable in APRV.
    - Persistent hypercarbia despite APRV = poor recruitment → unsafe.

14. **Hypotension**
    - Not an absolute contraindication.
    - CO best when lungs at FRC.
    - Watch early phase: high intrathoracic pressure until recruitment complete.

15. **Pneumothorax**
    - Not a contraindication.
    - Recruited lungs distribute pressure evenly → less strain per alveolus.

-------------------------------------------------
PRACTICAL NOTES
-------------------------------------------------
16. APRV requires clinical experience and practice.
17. Promotes physiologic ventilation aimed at restoring FRC.
18. Use minute ventilation trends as markers of recruitment.
19. With correct application, APRV may serve as:
    - Primary rescue mode for refractory ARDS.
    - Or, a conceptual framework to guide lung-protective ventilation.

SEVERE HYPOXAEMIC RESPIRATORY FAILURE
======================================

INTRODUCTION
-------------
- PaO₂/FiO₂ = bedside parameter for severity of hypoxaemia.
- Severe hypoxaemic failure: **PaO₂/FiO₂ < 100–150 mmHg**.
- Primary aim:
  → Ensure adequate gas exchange  
  → Minimise ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI).

-------------------------------------------------
PRINCIPLES
-------------------------------------------------
1. No proven outcome difference between **volume- or pressure-controlled ventilation**.
2. **Protective Lung Ventilation (PLV) strategy**:
   - Tidal volume ≤ 6 mL/kg ideal body weight.
   - Plateau pressure (Pplat) ≤ 30 cmH₂O.
   - Accept permissive hypoxaemia (lower PaO₂).
3. **Lung Recruitment Manoeuvre (LRM)**:
   - Reserved for patients who show **PEEP responsiveness**.
4. **Prolonged Prone Positioning**:
   - >16 hours/day recommended in early phase.
5. **Neuromuscular Blocking Agents (NMBA)**:
   - May be used early to improve synchrony and oxygenation.
6. **ECMO (Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation)**:
   - Consider as **rescue therapy** in refractory hypoxaemia.
   - Requires centre and clinician expertise.
7. **Avoid Non-Invasive Ventilation (NIV)**:
   - High failure rate when **PaO₂/FiO₂ < 200**.

-------------------------------------
I. PROTECTIVE LUNG VENTILATION STRATEGY
---------------------------------------

1. **Calculate Ideal Body Weight (IBW)**
   - Male:   50 + 0.91 × (height in cm – 152.4)
   - Female: 45.5 + 0.91 × (height in cm – 152.4)

2. **Ventilation Mode**
   - Pressure-Controlled Ventilation (PCV) OR
   - Volume-Controlled Ventilation (VCV)

3. **Tidal Volume (Vt)**
   - Target: ≤ 6 mL/kg IBW  
   - Plateau Pressure (Pplat): ≤ 30 cmH₂O

4. **PEEP (Positive End-Expiratory Pressure)**
   - Start with 10–15 cmH₂O.

5. **Driving Pressure (PCV mode)**
   - Keep ≤ 15 cmH₂O.

6. **Volume-Controlled Ventilation (VCV)**
   a. Measure Pplat every 4 hours (if passive ventilation).
   b. If Pplat > 30 cmH₂O:
      → Decrease Vt by 1 mL/kg increments  
      → Minimum Vt = 4 mL/kg.

7. **High Respiratory Drive**
   - If Vt > 6 mL/kg + ventilator dyssynchrony:
     → Consider NMBA (neuromuscular blocking agent) to prevent **P-SILI**  
       (patient self-inflicted lung injury).

8. **Oxygenation Targets**
   - Use **lowest FiO₂** to maintain:
     → PaO₂ ≥ 55 mmHg OR SpO₂ ≥ 88%.  
   - Allow **permissive hypoxaemia**.

9. **Permissive Hypercapnia**
   - Accept **pH ≥ 7.2**.
   - Increase RR up to 35/min if needed.
   - Contraindications:
     • Raised ICP  
     • Acute coronary artery disease  
     • Arrhythmia  
     • Right heart failure  
     • Worsening pulmonary hypertension

-------------------------------------------------
II. MEAN ALVEOLAR PRESSURE
-------------------------------------------------

1. **Optimise Mean Alveolar Pressure**

   a. **Increase Inspiratory Time (Ti)**
      - In VCV: ↓ flow rate or add inspiratory pause.
      - In PCV: Set Ti in seconds or adjust I:E ratio.
      - May require deeper sedation (risk of air trapping, barotrauma, haemodynamic instability).

   b. **Optimise PEEP and Inspiratory Pressure**
      - Aim for maximal alveolar recruitment with minimal overdistension.

-------------------------------------------------
III. LUNG RECRUITMENT MANOEUVRE & OPTIMAL PEEP SELECTION
-------------------------------------------------

1. **Common Non-Recruitable Pathologies**
   - Focal lung injury (e.g. contusion, focal pneumonia)
   - Pulmonary ARDS (low recruitability)

-------------------------------------------------
2. **Assess ‘PEEP Responsiveness’** (Evaluate recruitment potential)
-------------------------------------------------
   a. Increase PEEP → 15 cmH₂O for 30 mins.
   b. High recruitability if following observed:
      i. ↑ PaO₂/FiO₂  
      ii. ↓ PaCO₂  
      iii. ↑ Respiratory system compliance (Crs)  
          - Dynamic compliance (Cdyn) = Vt delivered / (Ppeak – PEEP)
          - Cdyn auto-measured in some ventilators.

-------------------------------------------------
3. **Non-Recruiters**
-------------------------------------------------
   - Do **not perform** recruitment manoeuvre.
   - Keep PEEP ≤ 10 cmH₂O.
   - Consider **prone positioning** + **muscle paralysis** instead.

-------------------------------------------------
4. **Recruitment Manoeuvre in Recruiters**
-------------------------------------------------
   a. **Preparation**
      - Additional sedation ± paralysis may be required.
      - Monitor for **hypotension** and **transient desaturation**.
   b. **Techniques**
      i. **Sustained High-Pressure Inflation**
         - CPAP 30–50 cmH₂O for 20–40 seconds (zero pressure support).
      ii. **Extended Sigh**
         - Stepwise ↑ PEEP and ↓ tidal volume over ~2 minutes.
         - Commonly used for VCV patients.

-------------------------------------------------
5. **PEEP / CPAP vs Duration Table**
-------------------------------------------------
   - Vt 6 mL/kg IBW → PEEP 15 cmH₂O → 30 sec
   - Vt 4 mL/kg IBW → PEEP 20 cmH₂O → 30 sec
   - Vt 2 mL/kg IBW → PEEP 25 cmH₂O → 30 sec
   - Vt 0 mL/kg IBW → PEEP 30 cmH₂O → 30 sec

-------------------------------------------------
III. LUNG RECRUITMENT MANOEUVRE (continued)
-------------------------------------------------

3️⃣ **Staircase Recruitment Manoeuvre (SRM)**
---------------------------------------------
- Mode: PCV
- Stepwise ↑ PEEP every 2 minutes; keep driving pressure constant.
- Peak Inspiratory Pressure (PIP): 45–50 cmH₂O.
- Driving pressure: 15 cmH₂O, RR 10/min, I:E = 1:1 to 1:0.
- Start with PEEP 20 cmH₂O.
- ↑ PEEP by 5 cmH₂O every 2 min → until PEEP 30–35 cmH₂O.
- Alternative: stepwise increase in PEEP with return to baseline between steps.

-------------------------------------------------
5️⃣ **Determining Optimum PEEP (after recruitment)**
-------------------------------------------------
a. Start PEEP at 20 cmH₂O → ↓ PEEP by 1 cmH₂O every 3 min  
   → Find maximal ↓ in PaO₂ or Crs = collapse point.
b. PaO₂ ↓ >10% OR Crs ↓ → indicates derecruitment.
c. Repeat recruitment with sustained high pressure (CPAP 30–50 cmH₂O for 20–40 s).
d. Optimal PEEP = 2 cmH₂O **above collapse pressure**.

-------------------------------------------------
6️⃣ **Post-Recruitment Assessment**
-------------------------------------------------
- If PaO₂/FiO₂ ≤ 150 mmHg → initiate deep sedation + prone ventilation.
- Consider NMBA.

-------------------------------------------------
IV. EARLY PRONE POSITIONING
================================================

1️⃣ **Indication**
- Severe hypoxaemia: place in prone position within 24 hrs.
- Maintain ≥16 consecutive hours.

-------------------------------------------------
2️⃣ **Contraindications**
-------------------------------------------------
**Absolute**
- Spine instability  
- Raised intracranial pressure (ICP)

**Relative**
- Severe haemodynamic instability / arrhythmias  
- Open abdominal wounds  
- Multiple trauma with unstable fractures  
- Pregnancy  
- Pulmonary haemorrhage  
- Facial trauma

-------------------------------------------------
3️⃣ **Monitoring**
-------------------------------------------------
- Measure ABG + respiratory compliance:
  • 1 hr **before** turning supine  
  • Within 4 hrs **after** returning to supine.

-------------------------------------------------
4️⃣ **When to Stop Prone Positioning**
-------------------------------------------------
- Sustained oxygenation improvement:
  • PaO₂/FiO₂ > 150 mmHg  
  • FiO₂ < 0.6  
  • PEEP 10–15 cmH₂O  
  → maintained for ≥4 hrs after turning supine.

-------------------------------------------------
KEY TAKEAWAYS
-------------------------------------------------
✔ SRM: gradual ↑ PEEP to 30–35 cmH₂O; identify collapse pressure.  
✔ Optimum PEEP = 2 cmH₂O above collapse.  
✔ Prone early (within 24 hrs) for ≥16 hrs/day.  
✔ Avoid prone if unstable spine or raised ICP.  
✔ Reassess oxygenation and compliance before/after each session.

BRONCHOPLEURAL FISTULA (BPF) – QUICK REFERENCE (up-to-date)
===========================================

- Communication between bronchus (main, lobar, or segmental) and pleural space.  
- Most common after pulmonary resection (pneumonectomy, lobectomy, segmentectomy).  
- Incidence: 4.5–20% after pneumonectomy, 0.5–1% after lobectomy.  
- Other causes: malignancy, infection.

-------------------------------------------------
CLINICAL FEATURES
-------------------------------------------------
- Acute: dyspnea, chest pain, tracheal deviation (tension pneumothorax).  
- Subacute: fever, cough with purulent sputum (empyema).  
- Persistent or new air leak via chest tube.

-------------------------------------------------
DIAGNOSTIC EVALUATION
-------------------------------------------------
- Based on clinical, radiographic, and bronchoscopic findings.  
- Imaging: air around bronchial stump, air in pleural fluid, pneumothorax, visible fistula.  
- Bronchoscopy: mucosal defect, air leak seen with saline test.

-------------------------------------------------
MANAGEMENT
-------------------------------------------------
- Rarely closes spontaneously; usually needs surgical or bronchoscopic repair.  
- Multidisciplinary management recommended.

General care:
  • Chest tube for air drainage.  
  • Drain and treat empyema with antibiotics and postural drainage.  
  • Optimize nutrition and comorbidities.

Postoperative BPF:
  • Surgical repair preferred for fit patients (definitive therapy).  

Non-surgical candidates:
  • Bronchoscopic closure for poor surgical candidates, severe illness, or malignancy.  
  • Temporary closure reduces morbidity and hospitalization.

Bronchoscopic techniques:
  • Success higher in small fistulas (<8 mm).  
  • <8 mm → occlusive materials or sclerosants.  
  • ≥8 mm or proximal → stents or Amplatzer device ± sealant.

-------------------------------------------------
FOLLOW-UP
-------------------------------------------------
- Monitor for recurrence via symptoms, air leak, and imaging.  
- Routine repeat bronchoscopy not required unless recurrence or complications suspected.

-------------------------------------------------
REFRACTORY CASES
-------------------------------------------------
- Repeat surgery or alternate bronchoscopic technique.  
- Rarely, open-window thoracostomy (Eloesser flap or Claggett window).

-------------------------------------------------
PROGNOSIS
-------------------------------------------------
- High morbidity and mortality (21–71%).  
- Best outcomes with surgical repair, poorer with postpneumonectomy empyema.

PULMONARY EMBOLISM (PE)
=========================

GENERAL OVERVIEW
-----------------
- Part of the spectrum of **Venous Thromboembolic (VTE) disease**.
- ~30% of patients with proven PE have coexisting DVT.
- ~50% of DVT patients have (often silent) PE.

INCIDENCE & OUTCOMES
---------------------
- Incidence: 60–70 per 10,000.
- Recurrence rate: up to 30%.
- Treated 30-day mortality: 5–10%.

-------------------------------------------------
RISK FACTORS
-------------------------------------------------
1. **Genetic / Coagulopathies**
   - Factor V Leiden mutation
   - Protein C deficiency
   - Antithrombin III deficiency
   - Antiphospholipid syndrome

2. **Hormonal / Endocrine**
   - Oral contraceptive pill
   - Pregnancy

3. **Virchow’s Triad**
   a. **Immobility**
      - Spinal cord injury
      - Prolonged surgery
      - Trauma (e.g. #NOF, long bone fracture)
   b. **Inflammatory state**
      - Surgery
      - Malignancy
      - Burns, trauma
   c. **Impaired blood flow**
      - Smoking
      - Obesity
      - Heart failure

-------------------------------------------------
PATHOPHYSIOLOGY
-------------------------------------------------

A. **Dead Space Ventilation**
   - Lung distal to embolus = ventilated but not perfused → ↑ dead space.
   - Causes inefficient gas exchange.
   - PCO₂ usually normal (due to compensatory tachypnoea).

B. **Shunt & Hypoxia**
   - Non-perfused regions → potential infarction.
   - Oxygenation via bronchial circulation persists:
     - Bronchial arteries → bronchial veins → pulmonary veins.
   - Increased flow through bronchial veins → physiological shunt → hypoxaemia.
   - If PFO present → paradoxical RA→LA shunt worsens hypoxia.
   - Alveolar collapse further aggravates shunt.

C. **Pulmonary Hypertension & RV Failure**
   - ↓ Pulmonary vascular bed area due to emboli.
   - Release of vasoconstrictors (TXA₂, serotonin).
   - Hypoxia induces **hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction**.
   - Result → **pulmonary hypertension** and **↓ RV output**.
   - RV dilatation → interventricular septum bulges → ↓ LV preload & compliance → ↓ CO.

-------------------------------------------------
CLASSIFICATION (AHA GUIDELINES)
-------------------------------------------------
**1. Massive PE**
   - Frequency: ~5%
   - Haemodynamic state: Unstable ± cardiac arrest
   - RV function: Dysfunction
   - Management: Thrombolysis

**2. Submassive PE**
   - Frequency: ~25%
   - Haemodynamic state: Stable
   - RV function: Dysfunction
   - Management: Anticoagulation ± Thrombolysis (if deteriorating)

**3. Non-Massive PE**
   - Frequency: ~70%
   - Haemodynamic state: Stable
   - RV function: Normal
   - Management: Anticoagulation

-------------------------------------------------
ESC GUIDELINES
-------------------------------------------------
- Use **PE Severity Index (PESI)** or **simplified PESI (sPESI)** for risk stratification.
- Stratifies patients by:
  - Clinical presentation
  - Haemodynamic stability
  - Comorbidities
  - Predicts short-term mortality

-------------------------------------------------
CLINICAL FEATURES
-------------------------------------------------
SYMPTOMS
---------
- Often asymptomatic (except for risk factors present)
- Dyspnoea
- Chest pain (pleuritic; distal pleural irritation from infarction)
- (Pre-)Syncope
- Haemoptysis

SIGNS
------
- Haemodynamic instability (shock, hypotension)
- Tachypnoea
- Hypoxaemia
- Low-grade pyrexia

-------------------------------------------------
DIAGNOSIS
-------------------------------------------------

A. **Clinical Scoring Tools**
------------------------------
1. **PERC rule**
   - Exclusion tool for low pre-test probability patients.
2. **Modified Wells’ Criteria**
   - Stratifies patients → further investigations (D-dimer or CTPA).

B. **Blood Investigations**
----------------------------
1. **D-dimer**
   - High negative predictive value → helps exclude PE.
   - Not validated in pregnancy.
   - Elevated in many other conditions (infection, inflammation, trauma, malignancy).
2. **Biomarkers of RV strain**
   - **Troponin:** poor specificity; elevation = poor prognosis.
   - **BNP:** marker of right heart strain and risk.

C. **Simple Tests**
-------------------
1. **ECG**
   - Most common: Sinus tachycardia (~40%)
   - May show:
     - AF
     - Right ventricular hypertrophy (RVH)
     - Right axis deviation (RAD)
     - RBBB
     - T-wave inversion (V1–V4)
     - S₁Q₃T₃ pattern (<20%)
2. **CXR**
   - Not diagnostic, but used for differential diagnosis.
   - Possible findings:
     - Hypovascularity (Westermark’s sign)
     - Peripheral wedge-shaped opacity (Hampton’s hump)

D. **Imaging and Other Tests**
------------------------------
1. **Doppler Ultrasound (Leg veins)**
   - High sensitivity/specificity for DVT in symptomatic legs.
2. **Transthoracic Echocardiography (TTE)**
   - Best for **massive PE** when unstable and CTPA not feasible.
   - Useful for non-massive PE with ECG signs of RV strain.
   - Shows:
     - ↑ RA pressure
     - RV dilation/dysfunction
     - RA enlargement
   - *Normal TTE does not exclude PE.*

3. **V/Q Scan (Lung Scintigraphy)**
   - Often inconclusive.
   - Superseded by **CT Pulmonary Angiography (CTPA)**.

4. **CT Pulmonary Angiography (CTPA)**
   - Contrast-enhanced scan.
   - Diagnostic finding: **filling defect** in pulmonary arteries.
   - Sensitivity: ~83%; Specificity: ~96% (NEJM 2006).
   - Optimised by:
     - Rapid scan time
     - Minimised motion artefact
     - Reduced radiation dose (useful in pregnancy)

5. **MR Pulmonary Angiography**
   - Pros: No ionising radiation; less nephrotoxic (gadolinium).
   - Cons: Lower accuracy; limited availability.

6. **Invasive Pulmonary Angiography**
   - Reserved for **pulmonary hypertension** or pre-**pulmonary endarterectomy** evaluation.

Continuous Renal Replacement Therapy (CRRT)
-------------------------------------------------

CRRT represents a family of modalities that provides continuous support for severely ill patients with AKI. These include continuous hemofiltration, hemodialysis, and hemodiafiltration, which involve both convective and diffusive therapies. It is intended to be applied for 24 hours or longer through continuous, slower dialysis. CRRT is particularly beneficial for hemodynamically unstable patients who require ongoing, large-volume fluid administration, such as multiple intravenous medications, or total parenteral nutrition. Because CRRT is a continuous therapy, the net solute removal over 48 hours is higher than with intermittent hemodialysis, despite the lower rate. CRRT acts as renal support through blood purification to allow solute and fluid homeostasis. It requires appropriate vascular access, pumps to allow blood circulation, a permeable membrane, and varying solutions to allow fluid balance. There are different techniques of CRRT that are distinguished by their method of solute removal.

Principles of CRRT:
• In CRRT, solutes are removed from blood by diffusion or convection. Different processes remove different sized molecules.
• Diffusion is the movement of solutes down a concentration gradient across a semipermeable membrane. This is the main physical process occurring during haemodialysis. Solutes (e.g. urea, creatinine) cross the dialysis membrane from the blood to the dialysis fluid compartment. Fluid in the dialysis compartment moves in a counter-current direction, thereby maintaining a concentration gradient. It is also possible for solutes (e.g. bicarbonate) to move in the opposite direction (e.g. from dialysate to blood).
• Convection – if a pressure gradient is set up across the dialysis filter, water is pushed across the membrane and carries dissolved solutes with it; this is known as the solvent drag. The movement of fluid across the membrane as a result of this transmembrane pressure is ultrafiltration, and the fluid produced is ultrafiltrate. The process by which solutes move across the membrane is convection.

Modality of CRRT:
• Continuous venovenous hemofiltration (CVVH):
o This method utilizes convection via a transmembrane pressure gradient to filter solutes.
o CVVH uses hydrostatic pressure to induce the filtration of plasma water across the hemofilter membrane. 
o Dialysate fluid is not used.
o The ultrafiltration flow rate is high (20 to 25 mL/kg/hour). As a result, replacement fluid must be given to prevent volume depletion. The amount of replacement fluid that is given is determined by the net volume removal that is desired.
o Small- and middle-molecular-weight molecules (ie, <5000 Daltons), such as urea and electrolytes, are removed in roughly the same concentration as plasma water. There is therefore no change in the plasma concentrations of these solutes by hemofiltration. However, the administration of substitution fluid lowers by dilution the plasma concentrations of solutes such as urea and creatinine that are not present in the substitution fluid.
o The removal of urea (and probably other small, lipid-soluble solutes) may also be increased by administering the replacement fluid before the hemofilter; this predilution lowers the plasma urea concentration, thereby allowing urea to diffuse from within red cells into the plasma water.

• Continuous venovenous hemodialysis (CVVHD):
o This method utilizes diffusion via a transmembrane concentration gradient across the membrane.
o Dialysate fluid is used. As in intermittent hemodialysis, dialysate fluid is run countercurrent to the direction of blood flow at a rate of 1 to 2 L/hour.
o By contrast to CVVH, the ultrafiltration rate is generally only 2 to 8 mL/min.
o The dialysate flow rate is 20 to 25 mL/kg/hour.
o In CVVHD, ultrafiltration is limited to the rate at which net fluid removal is desired, and no intravenous fluid replacement is required.

• Continuous venovenous hemodiafiltration (CVVHDF):
o CVVHDF combines diffusion with convection.
o CVVHDF requires infusions of both replacement fluid and dialysis fluid. Similar to CVVH, the ultrafiltration volume is variable, and replacement fluid must be given to maintain euvolemia. The amount of replacement fluid that is given is determined by the net volume removal that is desired.

• Slow continuous ultrafiltration (SCUF):
o SCUF is used to treat isolated fluid overload. 
o SCUF is not useful in patients who are uremic or hyperkalemic, because solute removal is minimal. 
o SCUF can safely remove up to 8 L of fluid per day. 
o Neither replacement fluid nor dialysate fluid is used.
o Convective solute loss is limited since the ultrafiltration rate is low compared with CVVH. There is no diffusive solute loss since dialysate fluid is not used.
o The blood flow is generally 100 to 200 mL/min and the ultrafiltration rate 2 to 8 mL/min.

Indication:
• Acute kidney injury (AKI) with:
o Fluid overload (refractory to diuretics)
o Hyperkalemia (K+ > 6.5 mmol L–1 or increasing rapidly)
o Severe metabolic acidosis (pH < 7.1)
o Rapidly climbing urea/creatinine (or urea > 30mmol.l-1)
o Symptomatic uraemia: encephalopathy, pericarditis, bleeding, nausea, pruritus
o Oliguria/anuria. (urine output < 200 ml/12 h)
o Serum creatinine > 400 μmol L–1
o Serum sodium < 110 or > 160 mmol L–1b
o Pulmonary oedema unresponsive to diuretics
o Symptomatic uraemia (encephalopathy, pericarditis, bleeding)

• Overdose with a dialyzable drug or toxin:
o Some drugs are removed by RRT but some are not. As a general rule, drugs are cleared by RRT if they are water-soluble and not highly protein-bound. 

• Severe sepsis:
o There has been recent interest in the potential for haemofiltration to remove inflammatory mediators in patients with severe sepsis/septic shock. 
o High volume haemofiltration (40-85ml.kg-1.hr-1) may reduce vasopressor requirements and possibly improve survival in patients with septic shock irrespective of whether they have an AKI.

Advantages of CRRT:
• Hemodynamic stability 
o Avoids hypotension, which is seen in ultrafiltration
o Avoids major swings in intravascular volume
o Allows slow and continuous tissue refilling
o Maintains steady cardiac filling pressures
o Avoids swings in intracranial pressures (beneficial in patients with raised ICP)
• Easy to replenish and regulate fluid volume
o Ultrafiltration is continuous and gentle
o Can adjust ultrafiltration rate according to hourly MAP status
o Can vary ultrafiltrate according to hourly variation in rate of infusates
o Can accurately adjust it to the intravascular blood volume or stroke volume variation when such monitors are being used
• Customize replacement solutions
o According to the metabolic parameters, replacement fluid composition may be altered like high lactate, calcium, high/low potassium, high/low sodium

Disadvantages of CRRT:
• Lack of rapid solute and fluid removal
• Glomerular filtration rate equivalent of 15–20 mL/min
• Limited role in drug overdose setting
• During filter clotting, entire system shuts down and the patient loses a lot of blood
• Necessitates continuous anticoagulation
• Limits mobility for various investigations
• Requirement of ultrapure fluids and high-flux dialyzers
• Costly

CRRT Procedure:

1.Vascular access:
• CKRT requires reliable vascular access capable of blood flows of at least 200 to 250 mL/min. The standard is a double-lumen tunneled or nontunneled dialysis catheter.
• The preferred vascular access site is the right internal jugular vein due to a more direct pathway to the superior vena cava. 
• An alternative to jugular access is femoral. 
• Subclavian is less preferred as it may result in stenosis of the vessel and may cause future issues if the patient requires an arteriovenous (AV) fistula. 
• End-stage renal disease (ESRD) patients who have AV fistulas should not use these if CRRT is needed. Using their established AV fistula increases the risk of needle dislodgment and bleeding.

2.Equipment:
• There are multiple integrated CKRT systems available. Their basic components are similar:
o Blood flow and dialysate inflow and outflow are controlled by roller pumps.
o Balancing systems provide ultrafiltration control.
o Dialysate inflow and outflow are continuously measured and the pump speeds adjusted to maintain the desired flow rates. Using this microprocessor-driven control, ultrafiltration rates can be maintained with high precision.

3. Prescription of CKRT:
• The prescription includes the choice of CRRT modality, method of anticoagulation (if this is used), filtration fraction, blood flow rate, dose, CRRT replacement or dialysis solution, and the fluid removal rate.

a.CRRT modality:
• CRRT modalities include continuous venovenous hemofiltration (CVVH), continuous venovenous hemodialysis (CVVHD), and continuous venovenous hemodiafiltration (CVVHDF).
• The modalities are distinguished by their underlying mechanism of solute removal. CVVH utilizes convection, whereas CVVHD utilizes diffusion. CVVHDF uses a combination of both convection and diffusion. 

b.Anticoagulation:
• In CRRT, the use of an extracorporeal circuit results in a prothrombotic state. If membrane clotting occurs, it will result in a decrease in the surface area available for diffusion or convection. As such, an anticoagulant is required to prevent clotting. 
• The anticoagulant used can either be systemic or regional. 
• The options for systemic anticoagulation include unfractionated heparin or low molecular weight heparin. 
• If a patient has heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), alternatives such as argatroban or hirudin may be used for systemic anticoagulation. 
• Regional anticoagulation is achieved using citrate. Citrate acts by chelating calcium and thus inhibiting the clotting cascade. If using this method, then serum ionized calcium levels should be monitored frequently. These patients will often require a calcium infusion with rate adjustments made based on the ionized calcium level. 
• One option for if anticoagulation cannot be used is intermittent saline flushes. This is performed every 15 to 30 minutes and helps wash off the fibrin strands. However, filter half-lives are generally reduced.

c.CRRT dose:
• The dose is defined by the effluent flow rate. The effluent is the waste fluid that comes out of the outflow port of the dialysate/ultrafiltrate compartment of the hemofilter. 
• For patients on CVVH, the effluent consists of ultrafiltration volume (ie, plasma water moved by convection across the hemofilter membrane). For patients on CVVHD, the effluent consists mostly of spent dialysate and, to a smaller degree, ultrafiltration volume generated by convection.
• We prescribe an effluent flow rate of approximately 25 mL/kg/hour in order to achieve (despite interruptions and CRRT downtime, which are inevitable) a minimum effluent rate of 20 mL/kg/hour over a 24-hour period. 
o Exceptions are patients with severe metabolic derangements (such as hyperkalemia or acidemia) that require more urgent correction over 24 to 36 hours. 
o For such patients, we start with a higher CRRT dose. For example, among some patients with severe metabolic acidemia (pH <7.1), we start with an effluent flow rate of 35 to 60 mL/kg/hour until acidosis is partially corrected.
o Once the severe metabolic derangements are improved, we decrease the prescribed CRRT dose to approximately 25 mL/kg/hour. 

d.Filtration fraction:
• The filtration fraction is the proportion of plasma water entering the dialyzer that is moved by ultrafiltration (convection) across the dialysis membrane. Stated differently, as defined above, it is the fraction of water that is removed from blood. 
• We maintain a filtration fraction <20 percent. 
• Higher fractions are associated with increased circuit clotting, presumably from hemoconcentration and blood protein-membrane interactions within the hemofilter.
• Filtration fraction is arithmetically defined as follows: 
o Filtration fraction  =  Ultrafiltration flow rate  /  Plasma water flow rate
o The ultrafiltration flow rate is the rate at which plasma water is transferred across the membrane driven by a pressure gradient between blood and dialysate/ultrafiltrate compartments.
o The plasma water flow rate is the rate at which plasma water is delivered to the dialyzer or hemofilter. It is equal to:
 The blood flow rate  x  (1 - Hematocrit) + The prefilter replacement fluid flow rate + Any other prepump infusion rate (such as citrate)
• A relatively low filtration fraction can be maintained by:
o Keeping the ultrafiltration flow (convection) rate low
o Increasing blood flow rate (which determines plasma water flow rate), providing catheter function can support higher flows
o Using prefilter replacement fluid in CVVH or CVVHDF

e.CRRT blood flow rate:
• For patients who are on anticoagulation, we try to maintain a blood flow rate of 200 mL/min (although blood flow rates of <200 mL/min are used frequently with regional citrate anticoagulation). 
• Among patients who are not on anticoagulation, a higher blood flow rate (250 to 300 mL/min) may be required to maintain catheter patency and CRRT circuit life. 
• Filtration fraction is inversely proportional to the blood flow. 
• Consequently, low blood flow rates (<100 to 150 mL/min) can increase hemofilter and circuit failures due to stasis of blood and an increase in filtration fraction.
• Blood flow rates higher than 250 to 300 mL/min may decrease the circuit lifespan. This is because the vascular access often cannot support these higher blood flow rates over many hours or days in critically ill patients. Over time, this results in increased access and return pressure alarms, temporary blood pump stoppage, blood stasis, and increased frequency of circuit clotting. 
• In general, for CVVHD, the blood flow rate should be ≥2.5 times the dialysate flow rate. This allows for complete saturation of the dialysate and preserves the linear relationship between dialysate rate and small-solute clearance. When using CVVH with postfilter replacement fluid, the blood flow rate should be ≥5 times the replacement fluid rate to optimize the filtration fraction. When using CVVH with prefilter replacement fluid, the blood flow rate should be ≥6 times the replacement fluid rate to optimize the solute clearance.
• Among patients who are anticoagulated with RCA, faster blood flows also increase the amount of required citrate.

f.CRRT solutions:
• Solutions used for replacement fluid and dialysate can either be custom compounded on site or purchased from commercial vendors. 
o Sodium: The sodium concentration in commercially available solutions ranges from 130 to 140 mEq/L. For most patients, the sodium concentration in CRRT solutions should be physiologic (ie, 135 to 140 mEq/L).
o Potassium: The potassium concentration in standard solutions ranges from 0 to 4 mEq/L. We use a 4 mEq/L potassium concentration for all patients except those with severe hyperkalemia (defined as >6 mEq/L in the absence of electrocardiogram [ECG] changes or elevated to any degree in the setting of ECG changes consistent with hyperkalemia).
o Bicarbonate: Standard solutions have a bicarbonate concentration ranging from 22 to 35 mEq/L. We use a "high" bicarbonate solution (ie, 32 to 35 mEq/L) in all patients except those who are treated with RCA. Among patients treated with RCA, we use "normal" bicarbonate solution (ie, 22 to 25 mEq/L). Metabolic alkalosis is a common side effect of RCA since administered citrate is converted to bicarbonate.
o Phosphate ─ Standard solutions contain either no phosphorus or 1 mmol/L phosphorus. We use phosphorus-containing solution if the serum phosphate is <4.5 mg/dL and phosphorus-free solution in all other patients. 
o Glucose ─ Standard solutions are either glucose free or contain between 100 to 110 mg/dL glucose. We, and most clinicians, use a solution with 100 mg/dL of glucose. 
o Calcium ─ Standard solutions are calcium free or contain 2.5 to 3.5 mEq/L calcium. We use calcium-free solution if RCA is used. We use a maximum calcium concentration of 2.5 mEq/L if the solution contains phosphorus. 

g.Patient fluid removal rate:
• We target an hourly fluid balance ranging from net even to net negative 200 to 250 mL/hour, thereby yielding a 24-hour fluid balance of even to negative 4 to 6 L. 
• Occasionally, we suspend patient fluid removal completely and allow an hourly net positive fluid balance; this is especially common in situations where there are high ongoing sources of nonurine, non-CRRT fluid losses such as from surgical wounds/drains, severe burns, active hemorrhage, etc.
• The net goal and the rate of fluid removal are determined by the clinical condition. Almost all patients requiring CRRT have some degree of volume overload, which contributes to mortality and morbidity. However, hemodynamic status usually dictates the rate of fluid removal, and clinical discretion is critically important. 
• Clinicians should monitor a patient's hemodynamic status and slow or suspend fluid removal if there are signs of intolerance (ie, decreasing cardiac output, a progressive and concerning increase in vasopressor needs, etc). Bedside tools (such as pulse pressure or stroke volume variability, bedside echocardiography, and inferior vena cava volume) may be used to assist in volume assessment if questions develop regarding the pace of fluid removal. 

Laboratory monitoring:
• We monitor electrolytes and acid-base status every 6 to 12 hours initially. 
o We check blood electrolytes at least every six hours and include sodium, potassium, chloride, ionized calcium, magnesium, and blood gas analysis, along with calculation of the anion gap. 
o At least once daily, total blood calcium concentration should be monitored to calculate the calcium ratio (total calcium/ionized calcium) or calcium gap (total calcium minus ionized calcium), which, if abnormal, may indicate citrate accumulation. 
o Blood gas analyzers may need to be recalibrated to measure low-ionized calcium concentrations.
• The need for monitoring anticoagulation efficacy in the circuit depends on the method of citrate delivery. If the dose of citrate is fixed in relation to the blood flow, monitoring is not necessary as long as blood flow is constant. If the citrate dose is not fixed to a constant blood flow rate, postfilter ionized calcium levels should be measured at least every six hours and the infusion of citrate titrated for an ionized calcium <0.35 mmol/L.
• If the patient remains stable with minimal changes in electrolytes at 24 to 48 hours, measurements of electrolytes can be decreased to every 12 to 24 hours.
• When using regional citrate anticoagulation (RCA), more frequent monitoring is required.

Clinical Significance:
• When to Initiate:
o There are many factors to consider when deciding whether to initiate CRRT. 
o Two main factors are the severity of illness and the necessity of the procedure. 
 The severity of the illness may be judged through the severity of the AKI and the observed trend. 
 Further supporting factors would include the presence of electrolyte and acid-base disorders, evidence of fluid overload, and other significant organ dysfunction that requires renal support for promoting recovery. 
o The necessity of the procedure may be judged through the assessment of the likelihood of the reversibility of the acute kidney injury (AKI), the presence of oliguria, and the nature and timing of the renal insult. 

• When to Discontinue:
o Every patient on CRRT should have daily monitoring for renal recovery. 
o There are no standards for dialysis discontinuation.

HEMATOLOGY SECTION
===========================================
POTENTIAL SOURCES OF EMBOLI – QUICK REFERENCE
===========================================

- Systemic (arterial) emboli usually arise from intracardiac or arterial locations.  
- Venous thrombus can cause systemic embolism in patients with intracardiac shunt (e.g. patent foramen ovale).

-------------------------------------------------
INTRACARDIAC SOURCES
-------------------------------------------------
- Must be left-sided in absence of intracardiac shunt.  
- Two main categories:
  • Thrombus  
  • Nonthrombotic masses  

THROMBUS
---------
- Occurs with low-flow states or mechanical prosthetic valves with subtherapeutic anticoagulation.  
- Locations and causes:
  • Left ventricle (LV):
    - After myocardial infarction (anteroapical, akinetic or dyskinetic wall).
    - Dilated cardiomyopathy (ischemic or nonischemic).
    - Stress (Takotsubo) cardiomyopathy.
  • Left atrium (LA) / Left atrial appendage (LAA):
    - Atrial fibrillation or flutter.
    - Mitral stenosis causing LA dilation.
    - Cardiac amyloidosis with impaired atrial mechanical activity.
  • Mechanical prosthetic valves:
    - Risk of thrombus with subtherapeutic warfarin.
    - Mitral or aortic position prone to embolization.
    - TEE preferred to assess for valve thrombus.
  • Paradoxical embolization:
    - Right-to-left shunt (ASD, PFO ± atrial septal aneurysm).
    - Allows venous thrombus to enter systemic circulation.

NONTHROMBOTIC MASSES
---------------------
- May arise in LV, LA, or on valves.  
- Types:
  • Tumors:
    - Myxomas (usually in LA attached to interatrial septum).
    - Papillary fibroelastomas (aortic or mitral valves).
  • Vegetations:
    - Infective endocarditis (native or prosthetic valves, or congenital heart disease).
    - Noninfectious (NBTE) due to malignancy, lupus, rheumatoid arthritis, sepsis, or burns.
  • Mitral annular calcification:
    - Rare embolic source via overlying thrombus formation.

-------------------------------------------------
ARTERIAL SOURCES
-------------------------------------------------
- Complex aortic atheroma (>4 mm thick or mobile) in ascending aorta or arch.  
- Source of thromboemboli or atheroemboli.  
- Seen in unexplained stroke, TIA, or peripheral arterial embolization.

==========================================
LEFT VENTRICULAR (LV) THROMBUS – QUICK REFERENCE
===========================================

- Risk factors:
  • LV systolic dysfunction  
  • Acute myocardial infarction (MI)

-------------------------------------------------
TESTING AND DIAGNOSIS
-------------------------------------------------
- Suspected embolism → perform echocardiography to identify cardiac source.  
- Initial imaging: transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) with contrast.  
- If inconclusive → cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging with long inversion time.  
- Cardiac CT: alternative but less available.  
- Transesophageal echo (TEE): option if CMR/CT unavailable; limited for LV apex visualization.

- Diagnostic features:
  • Protrusion into LV cavity, usually from apex.  
  • Independent mobility relative to myocardium = thrombus.  

- Acute thrombus:
  • Mural or pedunculated echodensity, mobile, similar acoustic texture to myocardium.  

- Older thrombus:
  • Echodense, layered or lamellar appearance.  
  • Often less mobile or organized.  

- Differential diagnosis:
  • Ventricular trabeculations  
  • Papillary muscle  
  • Near-field imaging artifacts  
  • Rare cardiac tumors  

-------------------------------------------------
PREVENTION
-------------------------------------------------
- LV systolic dysfunction without other indication → anticoagulation not routinely used.  
- Acute MI: prophylactic anticoagulation practice varies.  
  • Some clinicians avoid it even in severe LV dysfunction.  
  • Others use anticoagulation for apical akinesis or aneurysm formation.  
- Other conditions (e.g. specific cardiomyopathies) follow disease-specific recommendations.

-------------------------------------------------
TREATMENT
-------------------------------------------------
- Newly identified LV thrombus → anticoagulation for at least 3 months.  
- Reassess with imaging after 3 months:

  • No change in thrombus size → continue anticoagulation and reassess.  
  • Decrease in thrombus size or organized thrombus → continue anticoagulation if embolic risk remains.  
  • Resolution of thrombus but persistent risk factors (low EF, aneurysm, spontaneous echo contrast) → continue anticoagulation based on risk-benefit.  
  • Resolution of thrombus and improved systolic function, no aneurysm or other indication → stop anticoagulation and reassess periodically.

-------------------------------------------------
PROGNOSIS
-------------------------------------------------
- Embolic risk without anticoagulation: ~10–15%.  
- High-risk features: mobility and protrusion into LV cavity.  
- Possible outcomes: complete resolution, partial resolution, or endothelialized thrombus.

INFECTION SECTION

BACTERIA
===========================================
MYCOTIC (INFECTED) ANEURYSM – QUICK REFERENCE
===========================================

- Aneurysm = abnormal focal dilation of an artery.  
- Infection may occur in a pre-existing aneurysm or cause aneurysmal degeneration of arterial wall.  
- Source of infection often bacteremia or septic emboli.  
- Mycotic aneurysm term originally coined by Osler (described aneurysms in bacterial endocarditis resembling “fungus vegetations”).  
- Most mycotic aneurysms are bacterial in origin, not fungal.  
- Term “mycotic aneurysm” often used broadly, but classically refers to aneurysms due to arterial wall infection secondary to embolic material from the heart.

-------------------------------------------------
CLASSIFICATION
-------------------------------------------------
- True aneurysm:
  • Involves all three layers of arterial wall (intima, media, adventitia).  
- False (pseudo)aneurysm:
  • Blood leaks through arterial wall; contained by surrounding tissue.  

-------------------------------------------------
INFECTED ANEURYSM
-------------------------------------------------
- Serious clinical condition with high morbidity and mortality.  
- Occurs due to bacterial infection of the arterial wall.  
- May develop from:
  • Pre-existing aneurysm infection.  
  • Aneurysm formation secondary to infection.

-------------------------------------------------
TREATMENT
-------------------------------------------------
- Combination therapy:
  • Targeted antibiotic therapy.  
  • Surgical debridement of infected tissue.  
  • Vascular reconstruction when necessary.  
- Endovascular therapy:
  • Option for ruptured infected aneurysm.  
  • Used in patients at high surgical risk.

ANTIBIOTIC ANTIFUNGAL ANTIVIRAL

FUNGAL
==========================================
INVASIVE CANDIDIASIS (IC) – QUICK REFERENCE
===========================================

- IC includes:
  • Isolated candidaemia  
  • Deep-seated candidiasis  
  • Deep-seated candidiasis with candidaemia  

- Critically ill patients at high risk; mortality up to 55% if treatment delayed or inappropriate.

- Intra-abdominal candidiasis (IAC):
  • Most common deep-seated form.  
  • Often occurs without candidaemia.  
  • Risk factors: GI perforation, anastomotic leaks, necrotising pancreatitis, abdominal organ transplant.  

- Deep-seated candidiasis with candidaemia:
  • Seen in immunosuppressed and neutropenic patients.  

- Additional risk factors:
  • Parenteral nutrition  
  • Haemodialysis  
  • Broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure  
  • Candida colonisation (high Candida Colonisation Index increases risk)

- Candida albicans remains commonest species; rising non-albicans infections (e.g. C. glabrata, C. krusei).  
- Other invasive fungi (Aspergillus, Mucor) rare except in immunocompromised hosts.

- Diagnosis:
  • Blood culture – gold standard  
    - Sensitivity: ~75% in candidaemia; 5–20% in IAC  
    - Slow turnaround time  
  • Non-culture tests (adjuncts):  
    - Serological: mannan/antimannan, 1,3-β-glucan  (USM ADA!)
    - Molecular: Candida PCR  
    - T2Candida magnetic resonance assay  
  • Non-culture methods useful but costly and not widely available.  

- Persistent fever or candidaemia despite therapy → search for foci (endocarditis, catheter infection, abscess).

-------------------------------------------------
TYPES OF ANTIFUNGAL THERAPY
-------------------------------------------------
1. Prophylactic  
   - For asymptomatic high-risk patients.  
   - Start if ICU invasive candidiasis rate >5%.  
   - Indicated for recurrent GI surgery, anastomotic leaks, or transplant patients.  

2. Empirical  
   - For unexplained sepsis with risk factors for invasive candidiasis.  

3. Pre-emptive  
   - For unexplained sepsis with risk factors plus radiologic or non-culture evidence of invasive candidiasis.  

4. Targeted  
   - Initiated when culture and sensitivity results available.

===========================================
ROLE OF EMPIRIC ANTIFUNGAL THERAPY – QUICK REFERENCE *uptodate 2025*
===========================================
- In absence of bloodstream infection or proven invasive candidiasis, decision for empiric antifungal therapy depends on clinical context.

- Neutropenic patients with fever:
  • Empiric antifungal therapy routinely given (high risk for invasive candidiasis).  

- Nonneutropenic septic patients:
  • Routine empiric antifungal therapy not recommended in initial management.  

- Nonneutropenic critically ill patients:
  • Persistent fever or unexplained hypotension despite broad-spectrum antibiotics.  
  • Benefit of empiric antifungal therapy uncertain.  
  • Clinical criteria for initiation poorly defined.  

- 2016 IDSA guidelines:
  • Consider empiric antifungal therapy in critically ill patients with:
    - Persistent unexplained fever  
    - Risk factors for invasive candidiasis  

- Risk factors:
  • Indwelling central venous catheter  
  • Parenteral nutrition  
  • Hemodialysis  
  • Trauma or burns  
  • Broad-spectrum antibiotic use  
  • Recent surgery (especially abdominal)  
  • Positive serum beta-D-glucan assay  
  • Candida colonization at nonsterile sites  

- Recommended agents:
  • Echinocandin (preferred).  
  • Fluconazole acceptable if:
    - No prior azole exposure.  
    - Colonized with fluconazole-susceptible species.  

- Discontinue empiric antifungal therapy if:
  • No response after 4–5 days.  
  • No evidence of invasive candidiasis.  
  • Negative serum beta-D-glucan (high negative predictive value).  

- Evidence from clinical trials:
  • Micafungin (14 days) vs placebo in nonneutropenic ICU patients:
    - No improvement in infection-free survival at 28 days.  
    - Lower rate of new fungal infections.  
  • Micafungin vs placebo after intra-abdominal surgery:
    - No difference in invasive candidiasis incidence (11.1% vs 8.9%).  
  • Some earlier studies in bowel perforation/surgery:
    - Suggested possible benefit with empiric fluconazole.

===========================================
ANTIFUNGAL AGENTS – QUICK REFERENCE
===================================

- Common agents for candidemia:
  • Echinocandins: caspofungin, micafungin, anidulafungin, rezafungin (rarely used)  
  • Azoles: fluconazole, voriconazole, posaconazole, itraconazole, isavuconazole  
  • Amphotericin B formulations (used less often due to toxicity)

-------------------------------------------------
ECHINOCANDINS
-------------------------------------------------
- Includes caspofungin, anidulafungin, micafungin, rezafungin.  
- Mechanism: noncompetitive inhibition of 1,3-beta-D-glucan synthesis (fungal cell wall).  
- Fungicidal for most Candida species.  
- Broad-spectrum activity; favorable safety profile.  
- Approved for candidemia and invasive candidiasis.  
- Adverse effects: mild; fever, thrombophlebitis, headache, elevated liver enzymes.  
- Preferred first-line treatment for candidemia and invasive candidiasis (IDSA 2016).  
- Resistance increasingly seen in C. glabrata; high MICs in C. parapsilosis, C. guilliermondii.  
- Mechanism of resistance: mutations in FKS1/FKS2 genes; cross-resistance across the class.

-------------------------------------------------
AZOLES
-------------------------------------------------
- Includes fluconazole, voriconazole, posaconazole, itraconazole, isavuconazole.  
- Mechanism: inhibition of cytochrome P450-dependent lanosterol 14-alpha-demethylase → prevents ergosterol synthesis (fungal cell membrane).  
- Interacts with CYP450 enzymes → numerous drug interactions.  
- Preferred alternative if patient not on P450-dependent medications.

Fluconazole:
  • Excellent safety and bioavailability.  
  • Available IV and orally.  
  • Inexpensive (generic).  
  • Suitable for most patients if species susceptible.

Voriconazole:
  • Broader activity than fluconazole (lower MICs).  
  • Cross-resistance with fluconazole in C. glabrata.  
  • Active against C. krusei.  
  • Noninferior to amphotericin B in nonneutropenic candidemia.  
  • More toxicity; limited role as primary therapy.

Posaconazole:
  • Approved for oropharyngeal candidiasis and prophylaxis in transplant or neutropenic patients.  
  • Limited data for candidemia; marginal benefit over fluconazole.

Isavuconazole:
  • Not approved for candidiasis.  
  • Prodrug: isavuconazonium → isavuconazole.  
  • Failed to show noninferiority to caspofungin in trial (higher success with caspofungin).  
  • Limited role; marginal activity similar to fluconazole.

-------------------------------------------------
AMPHOTERICIN B
-------------------------------------------------
- Polyene antifungal; binds ergosterol, forms pores → leakage of cell contents.  
- Formulations:
  • Amphotericin B deoxycholate (conventional)  
  • Liposomal amphotericin B  
  • Amphotericin B lipid complex  
- Rapidly fungicidal against most Candida species.  
- Previously standard for candidiasis; now rarely used due to nephrotoxicity and infusion reactions.  
- Lipid-based forms less toxic than deoxycholate.  
- Reserved for cases with resistance to azoles and echinocandins.  
- Remains effective but with higher toxicity risk.

DIAGNOSTIC EVALUATION
---------------------
- Isolated candiduria without symptoms → difficult to distinguish:
  • Colonization vs local bladder infection vs upper tract (renal) disease  
- Urine findings (e.g. yeast count, pyuria) → do NOT differentiate infection from colonization.  
- Persistent candiduria (esp. in diabetics) → perform renal imaging:
  • Ultrasound or CT abdomen.  
  • Look for hydronephrosis, fungus balls, perinephric abscess.  
- If systemic signs (fever, sepsis) → evaluate for disseminated infection:
  • Imaging and blood cultures.

-------------------------------------------------
MANAGEMENT
-------------------------------------------------

A. ASYMPTOMATIC CANDIDURIA
---------------------------
- Usually no antifungal therapy needed.  
- Treat ONLY if:
  • Neutropenic patients.  
  • Very low birth weight infants (<1500 g).  
  • Before urinary tract manipulation.  
- Consider imaging for persistent candiduria in:
  • Diabetics.  
  • Structural/urologic abnormalities.  
  • Neonates (risk of fungus balls or abscess).

-------------------------------------------------
B. SYMPTOMATIC CANDIDURIA
--------------------------
- Always treat.  
- Empiric antifungal choice guided by species likelihood & past microbiology.  
- Tailor therapy once species identified.  
- If disseminated infection suspected → treat as systemic candidiasis.

1. Fluconazole-susceptible Candida
  • Cystitis → Fluconazole 200 mg (3 mg/kg) PO daily × 14 days.  
  • Pyelonephritis → Fluconazole 200–400 mg (3–6 mg/kg) PO daily × 14 days.

2. Fluconazole-resistant Candida
  • Amphotericin B deoxycholate 0.3–0.6 mg/kg/day IV × 1–7 days.  
  • Add Flucytosine 25 mg/kg QID (reduce dose if renal impairment).  
  • Flucytosine monotherapy (25 mg/kg QID) — option if non-hospitalized or renal impairment.  
  • Refractory cases → Micafungin (salvage, Grade 2C).

Note:
  • Lipid Amphotericin B formulations → ineffective (poor urinary concentration).  
  • Voriconazole, posaconazole, isavuconazole → not recommended (poor urine levels).

-------------------------------------------------
C. MANAGEMENT OF COMPLICATIONS
------------------------------
- Fungus balls / Perinephric abscess / Prostate abscess
  • Combine surgical drainage or intervention + antifungal therapy.  
- Renal transplant infections
  • Often need surgical or interventional management in addition to antifungal therapy.
