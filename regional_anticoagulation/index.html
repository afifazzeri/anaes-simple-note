Core Principles

Dose terminology — a key change

NEW Previous editions used prophylactic / therapeutic. The 5th edition uses “low dose” and “high dose” to align with other guidelines and reflect that the same milligram dose can be prophylactic in one patient and therapeutic in another.

e.g. the same “high” DOAC dose may treat a DVT in one patient and prevent recurrent DVT in another. Reduced renal function, low body weight, advanced age, or P-gp inhibitors (ciclosporin, dronedarone, erythromycin, ketoconazole) can turn a “low” dose into a “high” dose.

Pharmacokinetic rule of thumb

2× t½ Low dose → wait 2 half-lives (≈25% drug remaining) before neuraxial or deep plexus block.
5× t½ High dose → wait 5 half-lives (≈3–6% drug remaining) before neuraxial or deep plexus block.

Use renal-function–adjusted half-lives where relevant. Times assume normal renal function unless stated.

Overall philosophy

KEY ASRA PM takes an “antihaemorrhagic” stance — deliberately more conservative than thrombosis-prevention guidelines (ACCP, EHRA). The intent is near-complete resolution of antithrombotic effect before high-risk blockade.
KEY A neuraxial block is a high bleeding-risk procedure — not because of blood loss, but because bleeding occurs in a fixed, non-compressible space with potential for permanent neurological deficit.

Drug-specific assays

ASSAY Do NOT use PT or aPTT to assess residual DOAC effect — reagent sensitivity varies widely.
THRESHOLD Consensus safe haemostatic threshold: DOAC plasma level < 30 ng/mL or anti-Xa activity ≤ 0.1 IU/mL (dabigatran measured by dTT).

Risk factors (often additive, possibly synergistic)

1Age — especially elderly women (reduced vertebral canal compliance, osteoporotic deformity).
2Spinal pathology — cord or vertebral column abnormalities.
3Coagulopathy — existing or acquired (including HELLP).
4Difficult / traumatic needle placement — bloody tap was an associated factor in ~50% of spinal haematomas.
5Indwelling neuraxial catheter during continued anticoagulation — particularly UFH or LMWH. Half of IV-heparin–associated haematomas presented at the time of catheter removal.
6Concurrent haemostasis-altering drugs — the additive effect of multiple agents cannot be overstated.

Clinical pearls

PEARLPresentation is typically lower extremity weakness, not radicular back pain.
PEARLOutcome depends on speed of diagnosis and decompression — only 5 of 33 patients in the Swedish series recovered neurologically, largely due to delayed diagnosis/intervention.
PEARLObstetric patients have a markedly lower risk — likely due to hypercoagulability and a more compliant epidural space.

Even with full guideline adherence, ~13% of spinal haematomas have no identifiable risk factor. Protocols for urgent MRI and haematoma evacuation must be in place.

Supporting evidence

PAUSE trial (n = 3007): after a mean dabigatran-free interval of 63.2 h (CrCl ≥ 50) or 110.2 h (CrCl < 50), 98.9% had residual levels < 30 ng/mL. No spinal haematomas occurred among 230 patients who received a neuraxial block.

Pharmacology notes

INR is most sensitive to factors VII and X. INR 1.5 ≈ factor VII activity of 40%. INR ≤ 1.4 is generally not associated with increased spinal bleeding risk. Warfarin dose is inversely related to age and higher in women; renal insufficiency increases response.

Bridging

NO BRIDGEACCP recommends against heparin bridging for low-to-moderate thromboembolic risk patients.
BRIDGEConsider bridging if arterial thrombosis risk > 10%/year or VTE risk > 10%/month — start LMWH once INR falls below the lower end of target range. Use IV UFH instead in severe kidney disease.

Mechanical mitral valve, older-generation aortic valve, or aortic valve plus additional risk factors → bridging with LMWH is reasonable. Bileaflet aortic valve without risk factors → bridging can be avoided.

Heparins

General

RECDaily review of the medical record to determine concurrent use of other haemostasis-altering drugs (antiplatelets, LMWH, oral anticoagulants). IB
RECPatients receiving IV or SC UFH for > 4 days should have a platelet count assessed before needle placement (heparin-induced thrombocytopenia). IC

HIT typically onsets 5–14 days after initiation, but rapid-onset HIT can occur with re-exposure within 30 days; delayed-onset HIT may present days to weeks after discharge.

Intravenous heparin

STOPDiscontinue heparin infusion for a minimum of 4–6 h and confirm coagulation status is normal prior to neuraxial or deep plexus/peripheral block. IA
DELAYDelay IV heparin administration for a minimum of 1 h after needle placement. IA
CATHETERRemove indwelling neuraxial catheters 4–6 h after the last heparin dose (and after assessment of coagulation status); re-heparinise 1 h after catheter removal. IA

BLOODY TAPA bloody or difficult neuraxial needle placement may increase risk, but there are no data supporting mandatory cancellation. Communicate directly with the surgeon and make an individual risk–benefit decision. IA

Cardiopulmonary bypass

AVOIDNeuraxial anaesthetics are not recommended in the setting of high-dose anticoagulation and cardiopulmonary bypass. Estimated haematoma risk ≈ 1:1528 epidural and 1:3610 spinal, with no reduction in hospital stay, MI or mortality.

Practical notes

Anti-Xa peaks 3–5 h after administration; elimination half-life ≈ 5 h (up to 16 h in severe renal insufficiency). Protamine only partially reverses LMWH — anti-Xa activity is not fully neutralised and may rebound. Observational data show a significant proportion of patients still have detectable anti-Xa ≥ 24 h after high-dose enoxaparin; a 2023 trial found the time to fall below 0.2 IU/mL was 31.5 h.

Antiplatelet Agents
RECNSAIDs (including aspirin) do not create a level of risk that interferes with neuraxial or deep plexus/peripheral blocks. No specific concerns regarding timing of single-injection or catheter techniques, postoperative monitoring, or catheter removal. IC

Low-dose aspirin (81–325 mg/day) theoretically poses a greater bleeding risk than higher doses because higher doses also inhibit endothelial prostacyclin production. NSAIDs alone do not significantly increase spinal haematoma risk — but combination with UFH, LMWH, oral anticoagulants or thrombolytics does.

Exception: patients undergoing invasive pain procedures may be at higher risk. Celecoxib does not cause platelet dysfunction (COX-2 is not expressed in platelets). Non-aspirin NSAIDs produce a short-term defect that normalises within 3 days.

STOPSuggested interval between discontinuation and needle placement: 5–7 days for clopidogrel, 7–10 days for prasugrel. IIC

The shorter end of each range reflects labelling and the fact that most patients have significant (though partial) platelet recovery by then. Patients at high bleeding risk require the longer interval for complete recovery. A pharmacological study showed ≥ 90% return to baseline reactivity by day 6 (clopidogrel) and day 9 (prasugrel).

CATHETERNeuraxial and deep plexus/peripheral catheters should not be maintained with prasugrel (rapid onset).
CATHETERClopidogrel catheters may be maintained for 1–2 days, provided a loading dose is not administered. IIC
RESUMEThienopyridine therapy may be resumed immediately after needle placement/catheter removal, provided a loading dose is not given. If a loading dose is used, allow a 6 h interval between catheter removal and administration. IIC

Platelet function assays (PFA-II, P2Y12) can assess residual effect, but only a normalised value is useful — an acceptable residual level remains undetermined. Three spinal haematomas have been attributed to neuraxial techniques plus thienopyridines.

Previous guidance was 5–7 days. The new interval reflects labelling and pharmacological data on platelet function recovery (70% recovery at 3 days, baseline at 5 days). Note: ACCP recommends 3–5 days based on expert opinion; ASRA PM remains more conservative.

Thrombolytics & Other Agents
HISTORYIn patients scheduled to receive thrombolytic therapy, query the patient and review the medical record for recent lumbar puncture, spinal/epidural anaesthesia, or epidural steroid injection to allow appropriate monitoring. Original contraindications suggest avoiding these drugs for 10 days following puncture of non-compressible vessels. IA
AVOIDIn patients who have received fibrinolytic/thrombolytic drugs, recommend against needle placement for at least 48 h. Document normalisation of clotting studies, including fibrinogen. IA
MONITORIf a neuraxial block was performed at or near the time of thrombolytic therapy, continue frequent neurological monitoring (e.g. every 2 h) for at least 48 h after the last dose. If combined with an ongoing epidural infusion, limit the infusion to drugs minimising sensory and motor block. IC
CATHETERNo definitive recommendation for catheter removal if thrombolytics are given unexpectedly during infusion. Suggest measuring fibrinogen (one of the last clotting factors to recover) to evaluate residual thrombolytic effect and appropriate timing of removal. Continue frequent neurological monitoring for at least 48 h after removal. IIIC

Fibrinogen and plasminogen are maximally depressed at 5 h after thrombolytic therapy and remain significantly depressed at 27 h. Most published reports involve spontaneous spinal/epidural haematomas. Patients often receive concomitant IV heparin (target aPTT 1.5–2× normal) and an antiplatelet agent, further increasing risk.

AVOIDIn patients receiving parenteral thrombin inhibitors (argatroban, bivalirudin, desirudin), suggest against the performance of neuraxial techniques. IIIC

These agents inhibit both free and clot-bound thrombin. There is no antidote — the antithrombin effect cannot be pharmacologically reversed. Effect is monitored by aPTT and persists 1–3 h after IV administration. Although no spinal haematomas have been reported with neuraxial anaesthesia, spontaneous intracranial bleeding has occurred. Indications (typically HIT requiring therapeutic anticoagulation) make these patients poor candidates for neuraxial blockade.

NEWAvailable data suggest against the use of idarucizumab, andexanet alfa, PCC, or aPCC to reverse DOAC anticoagulant activity to enable the safe performance of a neuraxial intervention in routine patients. IIIC — NEW

Andexanet alfa (Andexxa)

Recombinant protein indicated for reversal of rivaroxaban or apixaban in life-threatening or uncontrolled bleeding. Reduces anti-Xa activity by ~92%. Not approved for reversal prior to emergency surgery/procedures or neuraxial interventions. Black box warning: arterial and venous thromboembolic events, ischaemic events including MI and stroke, cardiac arrest, sudden death. Short terminal half-life (5–7 h) means re-elevation or incomplete reversal may occur. Dosing: 400 mg (low) or 800 mg (high) IV bolus, followed by 120-min infusion of 480 mg or 960 mg respectively.

Idarucizumab (Praxbind)

Humanised monoclonal antibody fragment approved for dabigatran reversal in emergency surgery/urgent procedures or life-threatening bleeding. Complete reversal with dabigatran levels < 20 ng/mL within 30 min. Not FDA-approved for reversal prior to neuraxial interventions. Dose 5 g IV; an additional 5 g may be considered if bleeding reappears with elevated coagulation parameters. Risks: re-elevation of coagulation parameters, hypersensitivity, serious adverse reactions including fatal outcome in hereditary fructose intolerance (sorbitol excipient).

PCC / aPCC

May be an option if specific antidotes are unavailable or too costly, but all procoagulant agents carry inherent prothrombotic risk. No data evaluating their use for reversal before neuraxial intervention.

PRINCIPLEIf surgery can be postponed to allow spontaneous recovery of normal haemostasis, discontinuation of the antithrombotic drug is the preferred strategy. If waiting is not an option and reversal is needed for the safe conduct of surgery itself, regional techniques may be considered after full dabigatran reversal with idarucizumab.
Special Populations & Techniques
APPLYGiven limited pharmacological data in pregnancy and the absence of large series, apply the same recommendations in this document to parturients. IIIC
EXCEPTIONIn select high-risk parturients receiving VTE prophylaxis who require urgent intervention for maternal or fetal indications, the risk of general anaesthesia may exceed that of neuraxial anaesthesia — exceptions/modifications may be appropriate. IIIC

Why the risk is lower in obstetrics

DATAMoen et al: 2 spinal haematomas in obstetric patients with apparent coagulopathy (≈ 1:200,000). Bateman et al: 7 epidural haematomas among 142,287 obstetric patients (≈ 1:20,326) and none in obstetric patients.

Proposed explanations: the hypercoagulable state of pregnancy, a more compliant epidural space unimpeded by osteoporotic deformity (tolerates larger blood volumes before symptomatic compression), and pregnancy-related pharmacokinetic changes (lower peak anti-Xa, shorter duration of action, lower total drug exposure for LMWH).

Benefits of neuraxial anaesthesia for caesarean delivery

Maternal: reduces anaesthesia-related adverse events, reduces aspiration risk, avoids hypertensive response to intubation, avoids awareness, less intraoperative blood loss and uterine atony, fewer surgical site infections, superior analgesia with opioid sparing, reduced chronic post-delivery pain, enables immediate skin-to-skin bonding and breastfeeding.
Fetal/neonatal: more favourable Apgar scores and umbilical venous pH, avoids in-utero exposure to induction/inhalational agents.

Avoidable (non-emergent) general anaesthesia is associated with increased odds of severe overall complications (aOR 2.9; 95% CI 1.6–5.2) and severe anaesthetic complications (aOR 1.6; 95% CI 1.4–1.9).

Peripartum planning

PROTOCOLEvery labour unit should have a protocol for when anticoagulants should be stopped, and consider short-term strategies such as converting to UFH (shorter half-life) in anticipation of delivery. Multidisciplinary planning should determine anticoagulant dosing and cessation times.
DEEPFor patients undergoing deep plexus or deep peripheral block, apply the guidelines for neuraxial block similarly. IC
OTHERFor other plexus or peripheral techniques, base performance, catheter maintenance and catheter removal on site compressibility, vascularity, and consequences of bleeding should it occur. IIC

High-risk deep blocks (per Canadian Anaesthesiologists Society advisory)

Stellate ganglion, infraclavicular, lumbar sympathetic, lumbar plexus, paravertebral — plus other deep plexus/deep peripheral techniques. Haemorrhagic complications here, particularly with antithrombotic therapy, are often serious; significant blood loss rather than neural deficit may be the most serious complication.

Superficial nerve blocks can generally be performed without any therapy-free interval and irrespective of the antithrombotic dose — a position shared with ESAIC/ESRA. Published cases of clinically significant bleeding after peripheral techniques in patients with normal haemostasis continue to suggest vigilance.

ALERTEmergent antithrombotic therapy (vascular graft thrombosis, acute coronary syndrome/MI) or a communication breakdown can result in unanticipated anticoagulation with an indwelling epidural catheter. The acute pain service must be aware of alterations in the degree and timing of anticoagulation.
SYSTEMCentralised computerised pharmacy services can flag any antithrombotic order as a drug “interaction” and alert the pharmacist to contact the pain service — the “pharmacy failsafe”.
DECISIONTiming of catheter removal is based on the ongoing risk of thromboembolism, the need for continued antithrombotic therapy, and the potential for neuraxial bleeding during catheter maintenance and removal.

Use the drug-specific intervals in the relevant sections above when deciding how long to withhold the agent, or document an acceptable residual level (DOAC < 30 ng/mL, anti-Xa ≤ 0.1 IU/mL) before catheter removal.

SHAREDBoth use “low dose” / “high dose” terminology for DOACs, LMWH, UFH, fondaparinux and aspirin, since dose, indication and patient risk factors all influence pharmacokinetics.
VKAESAIC/ESRA recommend complete resolution of VKA effect with INR return to the local normal range (e.g. ≤ 1.1) — similar to ASRA PM’s requirement for a normalised INR.
DOACESAIC/ESRA recommend a high-dose DOAC-free interval of 72 h for all DOACs, with laboratory testing when renal function is impaired (anti-Xa inhibitors CrCl < 30; dabigatran CrCl < 50).
DIVERGENotable differences from ASRA PM exist for antiplatelet medications and apixaban. ACCP recommends ticagrelor be stopped 3–5 days pre-non-cardiac surgery, while labelling says 5 days and ASRA PM says 5 days.
PERIPHERALBoth agree: superficial nerve blocks need no therapy-free interval regardless of antithrombotic dose; deep nerve blocks follow the more stringent neuraxial recommendations.
OBSTETRICBoth apply the same recommendations as the non-pregnant population, with deviation possible in selected high-risk parturients after multidisciplinary discussion and risk–benefit analysis.
